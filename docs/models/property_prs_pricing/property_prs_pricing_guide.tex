\section{Introduction}
% =========================================================================

A \textbf{Property PRS} extends the Physical Risk Swap concept from a
river gauge to a specific property.  Instead of triggering on a gauge
water level, the contract references a named property and its flood
exposure.

\subsection{Why Property-Level Pricing Matters}

A gauge measures water level at a single point on the river.  Two
properties at different distances and elevations relative to that gauge
experience very different flood depths from the same event.  A property
on high ground 2\,km from the Thames may never flood even when the gauge
breaches severe, while a basement flat 200\,m from the channel floods
regularly.

Property PRS captures this distinction.  The fair spread reflects the
\emph{property-specific} flood probability, not the gauge-level
probability.  This is always lower than or equal to the gauge spread,
because the gauge is the most exposed point on the river.

\subsection{Gauge PRS vs.\ Property PRS}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
& \textbf{Gauge PRS} & \textbf{Property PRS} \\
\midrule
Reference entity & River gauge & Named property \\
Pricing method   & Analytical (hazard rate) & Event-count (Monte Carlo) \\
Term structure   & Upward-sloping & Flat (storms independent) \\
Basis to gauge   & Zero (is the gauge) & Positive (property safer) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\section{Event-Count Pricing}
% =========================================================================

Property PRS spreads are derived from the Monte Carlo storm catalogue
rather than analytical hazard rates.

\subsection{The Formula}

\[
  s_{\mathrm{bps}} = \frac{\text{severe floods at property}}{\text{total scenarios}} \times 10{,}000
\]

\noindent For example, if 45 out of 1,000 storm scenarios produce severe
flooding at a property, the fair spread is:

\[
  s_{\mathrm{bps}} = \frac{45}{1{,}000} \times 10{,}000 = 450 \text{ bps}
\]

\subsection{Why the Term Structure Is Flat}

Each storm scenario is an independent 168-hour event.  The probability
of a severe flood in any given year does not depend on the contract's
remaining maturity.  Therefore the property PRS spread is the same at
all tenors (1Y, 2Y, 3Y, 5Y).

This differs from gauge PRS, where the analytical survival function
$S(t) = e^{-\lambda t}$ naturally produces an upward-sloping term
structure (longer tenors have higher cumulative default probability).

\subsection{What Counts as a ``Severe Flood''}

A storm scenario produces a severe flood at a property when:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The nearest synthetic gauge's water level exceeds the severe
        warning threshold.
  \item The flood signal propagates to the property (accounting for
        distance, retention, elevation, and infiltration).
  \item The resulting flood depth at the property exceeds zero.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Extending to Wind: the Flood-or-Wind Union}

Each storm scenario is now paired one-to-one with a tropical cyclone, so a
single event can flood a property, blow it, or both. Because the spread is a
frequency count, adding wind is just a question of \emph{which events count}.
A scenario triggers the \textbf{wind} leg when the property's peak sustained
wind reaches its damage-onset threshold --- a threshold that is raised for
resilient (high-BRI) buildings, so a sturdier property counts less often. The
headline property spread becomes the \textbf{union}: count every scenario that
floods \emph{or} blows the property, dividing by the same number of scenarios so
the denominator is unchanged.

\[
  s^{\text{flood or wind}}_{\mathrm{bps}}
  = \frac{\#\{\text{scenarios that flood OR blow the property}\}}{\text{total scenarios}}
  \times 10{,}000
\]

\noindent The same catalogue yields four counts --- flood-only, wind-only,
flood-or-wind (the union), and flood-and-wind (the overlap) --- which always
satisfy $\text{union} = \text{flood} + \text{wind} - \text{both}$. These are
exposed as tradeable scenarios in the loan pricer (\texttt{win}, \texttt{fow},
\texttt{faw}), with BRI-anchored variants (\texttt{bow}, \texttt{baw}) that take
the flood leg at the resilient level the book trades at. A property in a
catchment with no tropical-cyclone exposure simply has an empty wind set and
prices exactly as the flood-only formula above. The full multi-peril mechanics
are in the PRS Pricing model document and the storm--typhoon coupling
specification.

\section{Basis Spread Decomposition}
% =========================================================================

The \textbf{basis} is the difference between the gauge spread and the
property spread:

\[
  \text{basis}_{\mathrm{bps}} = s_{\mathrm{gauge}} - s_{\mathrm{property}}
\]

The basis is always non-negative because the property is never more
exposed than its controlling gauge.

\subsection{What Drives the Basis}

Two physical factors create the basis:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item[Spatial Heterogeneity Effect (SHE)] Not every gauge-level
        severe event produces flooding at the property.  The spatial
        correlation model distributes rainfall unevenly, so some storms
        hit the gauge hard but miss the property's nearest synthetic
        gauge.
  \item[Surface Height Distance Effect (SHD)] Even when the synthetic
        gauge floods, the property may be elevated or far enough from
        the channel that the flood depth at the property is zero.
        Distance retention, terrain velocity, and floor level all
        attenuate the signal.
\end{description}

\subsection{Transmission Rate}

The \textbf{transmission rate} quantifies how efficiently gauge floods
translate to property floods:

\[
  T = \frac{\text{severe floods at property from this gauge}}
           {\text{severe events at gauge}}
\]

\noindent A transmission rate of 0.90 means 90\% of gauge-level severe
events also produce flooding at the property.  A rate of 0.30 means
only 30\% transmit --- the basis is large and the property is well
protected.

\subsection{IDW Reconstruction}

When a property has multiple nearest gauges, the property spread is
reconstructed from the gauge spreads minus basis via inverse-distance
weighting (IDW):

\[
  s_{\mathrm{property}} = \sum_{g} w_g \times (s_g - b_g)
\]

\noindent where $w_g = 1/d_g$ normalised to sum to 1, and $b_g$ is the
basis for gauge $g$.  In practice, the nearest synthetic gauge dominates.

\section{Property Trade Structure}
% =========================================================================

A Property PRS trade extends the gauge PRS CDM format with a
\texttt{PropertySet} block:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lp{8cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Field} & \textbf{Description} \\
\midrule
PropertyID      & Unique identifier (e.g.\ PROP-00012345) \\
PropertyAddress & Street address \\
Postcode        & UK postcode \\
LocalAuthority  & Council area \\
PropertyValue   & Market value (\pounds) \\
EAFloodZone     & EA flood zone (Zone 3a, 2, 1) \\
ReferenceGauge  & Nearest synthetic gauge ID \\
Latitude / Longitude & WGS84 coordinates \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The trade also carries a \texttt{GaugeBasket} with the controlling
gauge(s) and their weights, plus a \texttt{basis\_bps} field recording
the gauge-to-property spread difference at inception.

\section{Property-Level Greeks}
% =========================================================================

\subsection{Basis Delta}

The property FS01 is driven by the gauge hazard rate.  When the gauge
hazard rate moves by 1\,bp, the gauge spread moves by $\Delta s$ bps.
The property spread moves by the same amount (the basis is held
constant):

\[
  \text{FS01}_{\mathrm{property}} = \frac{\Delta s}{10{,}000} \times A \times N
\]

\noindent This is identical to the gauge FS01 because the basis is a
structural constant determined by the property's physical position, not
by market movements.

\subsection{Basis Risk}

The basis does change when the storm catalogue is regenerated (e.g.\
with different storm control parameters).  This represents
\emph{model risk}, not market risk.  It is not captured in FS01 but
is visible in stress testing.
