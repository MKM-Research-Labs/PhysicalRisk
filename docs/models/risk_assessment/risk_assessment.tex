% 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\mkmexecsummary

This document describes the Risk Assessment Model developed by MKM Research Labs for classifying and scoring flood-related risk at the property and mortgage level. The model provides a deterministic, rule-based framework that aggregates multiple risk dimensions---flood depth, loan-to-value ratio, property age, construction type, and proximity to water---into composite risk scores and qualitative risk classifications.

The model serves as the decision-support layer within the Physical Risk framework, translating quantitative hazard outputs (from the GEV Hazard Curve and Flood Risk models) into actionable risk categories for mortgage underwriting, portfolio management, and property-level risk reporting. Its outputs include flood risk level classifications, LTV risk categories, combined multi-factor risk scores on a 0--10 scale, property vulnerability assessments, value-at-risk estimates, insurance premium factors, gauge reliability scores, and distance-based risk decay factors.

The implementation is a single Python class (\texttt{RiskAssessor}) with stateless class methods, requiring no calibration or external dependencies. All thresholds and factor tables are defined as class-level constants, ensuring transparency and auditability.

% 2. MODEL PURPOSE AND SCOPE
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\mkmpurpose

\subsection{Objective}

The Risk Assessment Model provides the following capabilities:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item Classify flood risk level from projected flood depth into five categories: Very Low, Low, Medium, High, Very High.
    \item Classify loan-to-value (LTV) risk into four categories: Low, Moderate, High, Critical.
    \item Compute a combined multi-factor risk score incorporating flood risk, LTV, property age, and construction type.
    \item Assess property vulnerability to flooding based on elevation, projected flood level, and distance to water.
    \item Estimate financial value at risk as a percentage of property value.
    \item Calculate insurance premium factors based on flood risk level and depth.
    \item Assess gauge reliability from operational status and data frequency.
    \item Compute distance-based risk decay factors for proximity to flood sources.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope}

The model operates at the individual property or gauge level. It does not perform portfolio-level aggregation (which is handled by the Portfolio Flood Model) or spatial correlation modelling. All inputs are assumed to be point estimates; the model does not propagate uncertainty from upstream hazard models.

The risk classifications and scores are designed for internal use in MKM's Physical Risk reporting system. They are not intended as regulatory capital calculations, although the combined risk score can inform qualitative overlays on PRA-compliant capital models.

\subsection{Regulatory Context}

The risk scoring framework supports the bank model validation requirement for transparent and documented risk classification. Each threshold and factor is explicitly defined and version-controlled, satisfying the PRA SS1/23 requirements for model documentation and auditability.

\mkmframework

\subsection{Flood Risk Classification}

Flood risk level is determined by a simple threshold function applied to the projected flood depth $d$ (in metres):

\[
R_{\text{flood}}(d) =
\begin{cases}
\text{Very Low},  & d = 0, \\
\text{Low},       & 0 < d \leq 0.1, \\
\text{Medium},    & 0.1 < d \leq 0.5, \\
\text{High},      & 0.5 < d \leq 1.0, \\
\text{Very High}, & d > 1.0.
\end{cases}
\]

\noindent The thresholds are informed by UK flood damage research, where depths below 0.1\,m typically cause minimal structural damage, depths of 0.1--0.5\,m affect ground-floor contents, and depths above 1.0\,m may compromise structural integrity.

\subsection{LTV Risk Classification}

Loan-to-value risk is classified from the LTV ratio $\ell \in [0, 1]$:

\[
R_{\text{LTV}}(\ell) =
\begin{cases}
\text{Low},      & \ell \leq 0.6, \\
\text{Moderate}, & 0.6 < \ell \leq 0.8, \\
\text{High},     & 0.8 < \ell \leq 0.95, \\
\text{Critical}, & \ell > 0.95.
\end{cases}
\]

\noindent If $\ell > 1$ (input as a percentage), it is normalised by dividing by 100 before classification.

\subsection{Combined Risk Score}

The combined risk score integrates four factors into a single composite measure on a 0--10 scale:

\[
S = \min\!\big(10,\; S_{\text{flood}} \times m_{\text{LTV}} \times m_{\text{age}} \times m_{\text{constr}}\big),
\]

\noindent where each component is defined as follows.

\subsubsection{Base Flood Score ($S_{\text{flood}}$)}

The flood risk level is mapped to a numeric base score:

\label{tab:flood_scores}
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
\textbf{Risk Level} & $S_{\text{flood}}$ \\
\midrule

Low       & 2 \\
Medium    & 4 \\

Very High & 9 \\
Unknown   & 3 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsubsection{LTV Multiplier ($m_{\text{LTV}}$)}

\[
m_{\text{LTV}}(\ell) =
\begin{cases}
1.0, & \ell \leq 0.6, \\
1.2, & 0.6 < \ell \leq 0.8, \\
1.5, & 0.8 < \ell \leq 0.95, \\
2.0, & \ell > 0.95.
\end{cases}
\]

\subsubsection{Age Factor ($m_{\text{age}}$)}

\[
m_{\text{age}}(a) =
\begin{cases}
1.0, & a \leq 50, \\
1.1, & 50 < a \leq 100, \\
1.3, & a > 100,
\end{cases}
\]

\noindent where $a$ is the property age in years. Older properties are assumed to have less flood-resistant construction.

\subsubsection{Construction Factor ($m_{\text{constr}}$)}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Construction type risk factors.}
\label{tab:constr_factors}
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
\textbf{Construction Type} & $m_{\text{constr}}$ \\
\midrule
Steel    & 0.8 \\
Concrete & 0.9 \\
Brick    & 1.0 \\
Timber / Wood & 1.3 \\
Other / Unknown & 1.0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent The composite score is capped at 10.0 to maintain a bounded scale.

\subsection{Property Vulnerability Assessment}

The vulnerability score $V$ combines flood depth with proximity to water:

\[
V = \min\!\Big(100,\; d \times 30 + 50 \cdot \mathbf{1}(d > 0) + 20 \cdot \mathbf{1}(\delta < 1.0)\Big),
\]

\noindent where $d = \max(0,\, L_{\text{flood}} - E_{\text{ground}})$ is the projected flood depth (flood level minus ground elevation), and $\delta$ is the distance to the nearest water body in kilometres. The three terms represent:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $d \times 30$: linear depth penalty (30 points per metre of flooding);
    \item $50 \cdot \mathbf{1}(d > 0)$: step penalty for any flooding at all;
    \item $20 \cdot \mathbf{1}(\delta < 1.0)$: proximity bonus for properties within 1\,km of water.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Value at Risk}

The financial value at risk (VaR) is estimated as a percentage of property value $P$:

\[
\text{VaR} = P \times \max\!\big(r_{\text{base}},\; r_{\text{depth}}\big),
\]

\noindent where $r_{\text{base}}$ is a risk-level-based percentage and $r_{\text{depth}}$ is a depth-based percentage (used when flood depth is available):

\midrule

Low       & 5\%  & $0.5 < d \leq 1.0$\,m & 25\% \\
Medium    & 15\% & $1.0 < d \leq 2.0$\,m & 50\% \\
High      & 35\% & $d > 2.0$\,m & 80\% \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Insurance Premium Factor}

The estimated annual insurance premium as a fraction of property value is:

\[
\pi = \min\!\Big(0.10,\; \beta(R) \times (1 + 0.5\, d)\Big),
\]

\noindent where $\beta(R)$ is a base rate determined by the flood risk level:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Insurance premium base rates.}
\label{tab:insurance_rates}
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
\textbf{Risk Level} & $\beta$ \\
\midrule
Very Low  & 0.10\% \\
Low       & 0.20\% \\
Medium    & 0.50\% \\
High      & 1.50\% \\
Very High & 3.50\% \\
Unknown   & 0.50\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent The premium is capped at 10\% of property value to prevent unrealistic estimates for extreme scenarios.

\subsection{Distance Risk Decay}

The risk factor based on distance $\delta$ (km) to the nearest flood source follows a step function:

\[
\rho(\delta) =
\begin{cases}
1.0, & \delta \leq 0.1, \\
0.8, & 0.1 < \delta \leq 0.5, \\
0.6, & 0.5 < \delta \leq 1.0, \\
0.4, & 1.0 < \delta \leq 2.0, \\
0.2, & 2.0 < \delta \leq 5.0, \\
0.1, & \delta > 5.0.
\end{cases}
\]

\subsection{Gauge Reliability Score}

Gauge reliability is scored on a 0--100 scale based on operational status and data collection frequency:

\[
G = \max\!\big(0,\; \min(100,\; G_{\text{status}} + \Delta G_{\text{freq}})\big),
\]

\noindent where $G_{\text{status}}$ is the base score from operational status and $\Delta G_{\text{freq}}$ is an adjustment for data frequency:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Gauge reliability score components.}
\label{tab:gauge_reliability}
\begin{tabular}{lc|lc}
\toprule
\textbf{Operational Status} & $G_{\text{status}}$ & \textbf{Data Frequency} & $\Delta G_{\text{freq}}$ \\
\midrule
Fully operational      & 95  & Real-time & 0 \\
Maintenance required   & 70  & Hourly    & $-5$ \\
Temporarily offline    & 30  & Daily     & $-15$ \\
Decommissioned         & 0   & Weekly    & $-30$ \\
Unknown                & 50  & Monthly   & $-50$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent The score is classified into five reliability categories: Highly Reliable ($\geq 90$), Reliable ($\geq 70$), Moderately Reliable ($\geq 50$), Low Reliability ($\geq 30$), and Unreliable ($< 30$).

\subsection{Calibration}

All thresholds and factor tables are defined as class-level constants within \texttt{RiskAssessor}. There are no parameters estimated from data; the model is fully deterministic. The threshold values were chosen based on:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Flood depth thresholds}: Aligned with Environment Agency flood damage guidance and Multi-Coloured Manual depth-damage curves.
    \item \textbf{LTV thresholds}: Standard UK mortgage market conventions (60\% low-risk, 80\% standard, 95\% high-LTV).
    \item \textbf{Construction factors}: Relative vulnerability rankings from insurance industry actuarial tables.
    \item \textbf{Insurance base rates}: Calibrated to approximate Flood Re scheme pricing tiers.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Class Design}

The \texttt{RiskAssessor} class uses the utility class pattern: all methods are class methods that take inputs as arguments and return results without maintaining state. This design ensures:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Thread safety}: No shared mutable state.
    \item \textbf{Testability}: Each method can be tested in isolation with known inputs.
    \item \textbf{Auditability}: The same inputs always produce the same outputs.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Null Handling}

All assessment methods handle \texttt{None} inputs gracefully:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{assess\_flood\_risk\_level(None)} returns \texttt{"Unknown"}.
    \item \texttt{assess\_ltv\_risk\_level(None)} returns \texttt{"Unknown"}.
    \item \texttt{assess\_property\_vulnerability(None, ...)} returns a dictionary with null values and an insufficient-data recommendation.
    \item \texttt{calculate\_value\_at\_risk(None, ...)} returns 0.0.
    \item \texttt{calculate\_distance\_risk\_factor(None)} returns 0.5 (neutral default).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Recommendation Engine}

The \texttt{\_generate\_recommendations()} method produces context-sensitive recommendations based on flood depth and risk level. Recommendations are tiered:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High/Very High risk}: Flood insurance, flood-resistant modifications, evacuation planning, flood barriers. Additional recommendations for depths $> 1.0$\,m include relocating utilities and installing flood vents.
    \item \textbf{Medium risk}: Insurance review, flood warning monitoring, emergency supplies.
    \item \textbf{Low risk}: General awareness and seasonal monitoring.
    \item \textbf{Very Low/No risk}: Climate projection monitoring.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Threshold Sensitivity}

The risk classifications are step functions, meaning small changes in input values near threshold boundaries can produce discontinuous jumps in risk category. For example:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A flood depth of 0.49\,m is classified as ``Medium'' ($S_{\text{flood}} = 4$), while 0.51\,m is ``High'' ($S_{\text{flood}} = 7$), a 75\% increase in the base score from a 4\% change in depth.
    \item An LTV of 0.79 is ``Moderate'' ($m_{\text{LTV}} = 1.2$) while 0.81 is ``High'' ($m_{\text{LTV}} = 1.5$), a 25\% multiplier increase.
\end{itemize}

\noindent This boundary sensitivity is an inherent feature of threshold-based classification. Users should be aware that properties near classification boundaries may be sensitive to small changes in upstream model outputs.

\subsection{Combined Score Amplification}

The multiplicative structure of the combined risk score means that extreme values in multiple factors can amplify rapidly. For example, a property with:

\begin{itemize}
    \item High flood risk ($S_{\text{flood}} = 7$),
    \item LTV of 96\% ($m_{\text{LTV}} = 2.0$),
    \item Age 120 years ($m_{\text{age}} = 1.3$),
    \item Timber construction ($m_{\text{constr}} = 1.3$),
\end{itemize}

\noindent would produce an uncapped score of $7 \times 2.0 \times 1.3 \times 1.3 = 23.66$, which is capped at 10.0. The 10.0 cap ensures a bounded scale but means that very high-risk properties are pooled together without further differentiation.

\subsection{VaR Dual-Source Sensitivity}

The VaR calculation takes the maximum of risk-level-based and depth-based percentages. This means:

\begin{itemize}
    \item For properties with low flood depth but elevated risk level (e.g.\ due to proximity), the risk-level percentage dominates.
    \item For properties with high flood depth, the depth-based percentage may exceed the risk-level percentage, providing a more conservative estimate.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Unit Test Coverage}

The \texttt{RiskAssessor} class is validated through a comprehensive unit test suite covering:

\begin{itemize}
    \item All threshold boundaries for flood risk and LTV classification (exact boundary values and $\pm\epsilon$ tests).
    \item Combined score computation with known factor values and verification of the 10.0 cap.
    \item Vulnerability assessment with and without distance-to-water input.
    \item VaR calculation across all risk levels and with/without flood depth.
    \item Insurance premium calculation and 10\% cap verification.
    \item Gauge reliability scoring across all operational status and frequency combinations.
    \item Distance risk decay at each step boundary.
    \item Null input handling for all methods.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cross-Validation Against Expert Judgement}

Risk classifications have been reviewed against expert flood risk assessments for a sample of properties in the Thames catchment. The model's flood risk levels agree with Environment Agency flood zone designations in approximately 85\% of cases, with disagreements concentrated at the Low/Medium boundary where the EA's broader zone designations do not capture micro-topographic variations.

\subsection{Consistency Checks}

The following monotonicity properties are verified:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Flood risk level is weakly monotonically increasing in flood depth.
    \item LTV risk level is weakly monotonically increasing in LTV ratio.
    \item Combined risk score is weakly monotonically increasing in each factor (holding others constant).
    \item Vulnerability score is weakly monotonically increasing in flood depth.
    \item Insurance premium is weakly monotonically increasing in flood depth and risk level.
    \item Distance risk factor is weakly monotonically decreasing in distance.
\end{itemize}

% 8. MODEL LIMITATIONS AND KNOWN WEAKNESSES
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\mkmlimitations

\subsection{Deterministic Threshold Classification}

The model uses hard thresholds without any smoothing, interpolation, or probabilistic transition. This produces discontinuous risk classifications at boundary values, which may not reflect the gradual nature of flood risk. A logistic or sigmoid transition function would provide smoother classifications but at the cost of increased complexity and reduced interpretability.

\subsection{No Interaction Effects}

The combined risk score uses a simple multiplicative structure that does not capture interaction effects between risk factors. For example, the model does not account for the possibility that timber construction in a high-flood-risk area may be disproportionately more vulnerable than the product of independent factors would suggest.

\subsection{Static Factor Tables}

All thresholds and factor values are fixed constants. The model does not adapt to:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Regional variations in flood damage relationships (e.g.\ clay vs sandy soils).
    \item Changes in building regulations or flood defence standards over time.
    \item Property-specific modifications (e.g.\ property-level protection measures, raised floor levels).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{No Uncertainty Propagation}

The model produces point estimates for all outputs. It does not propagate uncertainty from upstream hazard models (GEV parameter uncertainty, flood depth estimation error) through to the risk scores. As a result, the precision of the output may overstate the actual confidence in the risk assessment.

\subsection{Insurance Premium Approximation}

The insurance premium factor is a simplified estimate based on risk level and depth. It does not account for:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Claims history and loss experience.
    \item Specific policy terms, deductibles, and coverage limits.
    \item Reinsurance market conditions and Flood Re subsidy eligibility.
    \item Property-specific mitigation measures that may reduce premiums.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The premium factor should be treated as an indicative estimate for comparative purposes, not as an actuarial pricing output.

\subsection{Recommendation Engine Limitations}

The recommendation engine is rule-based and produces generic advice. It does not consider property-specific circumstances, local authority flood action plans, or the availability and cost of recommended mitigation measures.

\mkmgovernance

\mkmvqitem{1}{Ronseal Test}{Does the model do what it says on the tin?}
Yes. The model classifies and scores flood-related risk at the property and mortgage level, delivering: (i)~flood risk level classification from depth (five categories), (ii)~LTV risk classification (four categories), (iii)~combined multi-factor risk score on a 0--10 scale, (iv)~property vulnerability assessment, (v)~value-at-risk estimates, (vi)~insurance premium factors, (vii)~gauge reliability scoring, and (viii)~distance-based risk decay factors. All outputs are produced as documented.

\mkmvqitem{2}{Accuracy and Stability}{Does the model provide timely, accurate and stable results?}
The model is fully deterministic and rule-based: identical inputs always produce identical risk classifications and scores. All thresholds and factor tables are defined as class-level constants, ensuring reproducibility and auditability. The classification thresholds are informed by UK flood damage research and industry-standard LTV risk boundaries. The stateless class method design means no internal state can drift between calls. Computation is instantaneous ($<$1\,ms per property).

\mkmvqitem{3}{Resource Usage}{Does the model consume excessive computational resources?}
No. The model is implemented as a single Python class with stateless class methods requiring no calibration, optimisation, or numerical solving. All functions are $O(1)$ per property (simple threshold comparisons and table lookups). The model has no external dependencies. Memory usage is limited to the class-level constant tables (fewer than 50 entries total). Portfolio-level scoring of 10,000 properties completes in under 100\,ms.

\mkmvqitem{4}{Override Controls}{Are there controls over manual overrides?}
All risk thresholds, factor weights, and scoring parameters are defined as immutable class-level constants. No manual override mechanism exists for individual risk scores or classifications. Changes to thresholds or weights require code modification subject to version control and code review. The transparency of the rule-based approach means that any score can be fully explained by tracing the input values through the published threshold tables.

\mkmvqitem{5}{Output Consumption}{How is the output consumed for risk decision-making?}
Risk classifications and composite scores are consumed by: (a)~the interactive governance dashboard for property-level and portfolio-level risk visualisation; (b)~mortgage underwriting processes (combined risk score informs approval decisions); (c)~portfolio management for geographic diversification monitoring; (d)~PDF property risk reports presented to the MRC; (e)~insurance premium factor calculation. The model's outputs are not used directly for regulatory capital calculation but inform qualitative overlays.

\mkmvqitem{6}{Model Chaining}{Is the model's output used as input to another model?}
Yes. The model is a downstream consumer and a terminal output model. Upstream dependencies: GEV Hazard Curve Model (MKM-HAZARD-001) and Flood Risk Model (MKM-FLOOD-001) for flood depth and hazard inputs; Property Valuation Engine (MKM-PROP-001) for property values and characteristics. Downstream consumers: risk reporting engine, governance dashboard, and PDF generation suite. The model's risk scores do not feed into other quantitative models but inform decision-making processes.

\mkmvqitem{7}{Weaknesses}{Does the model have weaknesses and limitations of use?}
Yes; the principal limitations are documented in Section~8. Key weaknesses: (i)~point-estimate inputs only (no uncertainty propagation from upstream models); (ii)~no portfolio-level aggregation (individual property scope only); (iii)~deterministic rule-based scoring may not capture non-linear risk interactions; (iv)~fixed threshold tables may require recalibration for different geographic contexts; (v)~flood risk is the only natural hazard considered.

\mkmvqitem{8}{Monitoring}{What type of monitoring is required?}
Monitoring should include: (a)~annual review of flood risk classification thresholds against updated EA flood damage data; (b)~LTV threshold review against mortgage market default rate data; (c)~comparison of combined risk score distribution against portfolio loss experience; (d)~gauge reliability score validation against EA gauge operational reports; (e)~insurance premium factor benchmarking against ABI claims data; (f)~review of the equal-weighting assumption in the multi-factor score against empirical risk factor importance.

\mkmvqitem{9}{Overall Risk}{Is the overall model risk acceptable?}
Acceptable. As a Tier~1 model, the Risk Assessment Engine has high visibility in decision-making. However, its rule-based design with transparent, published thresholds mitigates model risk: any output can be fully explained and reproduced from inputs. The primary risks are: (i)~threshold staleness (mitigated by annual review) and (ii)~simplistic equal weighting in the composite score (accepted by the MRC pending empirical calibration exercise). The model's stateless, dependency-free implementation eliminates operational risk.
