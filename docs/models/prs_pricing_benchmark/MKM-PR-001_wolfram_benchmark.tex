\mkmexecsummary
% =========================================================================

This document benchmarks the PRS Analytical Pricing Model (MKM-PR-001): the
function that turns a gauge's annual trigger probability into the fair spread of
a Physical Risk Swap. It asks two questions, and answers each against references
that share none of the model's numerics.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Does the code compute the documented formula?} The model document
  (\texttt{prs\_pricing.pdf}, \S3.1) specifies the spread exactly. Over \bmCases{}
  cases --- probabilities from $10^{-4}$ to the cap, tenors of 1 to 10 years, two
  recoveries, a flat rate and the book's yield curve --- the model agrees with a
  50-digit implementation of that formula to \bmWorst{} (tolerance \bmTol).
  \bmWolframConfirms{} The floor, the zero rule, the cap and the curve
  interpolation behave as documented.
\item \textbf{Does the model document describe the model accurately?} Three of its
  statements are numerical, and the benchmark tests them as written
  (Table~\ref{tab:bm-claims}). Two are not met; the third is a finding.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Findings.} The implementation is correct. The documentation is not:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Discretisation is understated (\S8.3).} The quarterly scheme prices
  every spread higher than continuous time by a factor of about
  $1+(\lambda+r)\Delta t/2$, at every tenor. That is \bmDiscLow{} bp at worst for
  probabilities up to 0.20, where the document says the scheme is accurate, and
  \bmDiscHigh{} bp above, where it says ``up to 2--3 bp''. At UK flood
  probabilities (around 1--2\%) the overstatement is about a bp
  (Table~\ref{tab:bm-worked}).
\item \textbf{The curve is no longer flat (\S8.4).} The document says the
  discount curve is flat; the book prices on a term structure. The effect is
  below 1 bp for probabilities under \bmCurveOver, as the document says, but
  reaches \bmCurveWorst{} bp.
\item \textbf{The basis compares two different formulae (\S3.3).} The basis code
  takes the gauge leg as $p\times10^4$ and the property leg from Eq.~7. At the
  same probability the two legs differ, so part of every reported basis is an
  artefact of the formulae: over 1 bp from $p=\bmBasisOver$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Evidence.} \bmChecks{} checks, outcome: \bmOutcome{}
(Table~\ref{tab:bm-checks}).

\subsection{What is benchmarked}
\texttt{compute\_prs\_spread} and \texttt{interpolate\_yield\_rate} in
\texttt{src/models/hazard/prs\_analytical.py}: the fair spread every PRS in the
book is priced at, and the curve it is discounted on. The module is loaded from
its file alone, and called exactly as the book calls it.

\subsection{What is not}
The basis waterfall's bookkeeping (counts, distances, inverse-distance weights)
is not a calculation a mathematical oracle can check, and is out of scope; only
the consistency of its two spread legs is examined. The delta engine (MKM-DE-001)
and the XVA overlay (MKM-XV-001) consume this function and are benchmarked
separately. This is a benchmark of the implementation (validation question Q8),
not independent validation (Q9).

\mkmframework
% =========================================================================

Following the model document (\S3.1): the annual probability $p$, capped at
0.999, gives the hazard rate $\lambda=-\ln(1-p)$; survival is
$S(t)=e^{-\lambda t}$ and discounting $D(t)=e^{-r(t)\,t}$. With quarterly dates
$t_i=i\,\Delta t$, $\Delta t=\tfrac14$, $n=4T$,
\begin{equation}
A=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta t\,S(t_i)\,D(t_i),\qquad
P=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-R)\bigl(S(t_{i-1})-S(t_i)\bigr)\,D\!\left(t_i-\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\right),
\end{equation}
and the fair spread is $s=10^4\,P/A$ bp, floored at \bmFloor{} bp. The rate $r(t)$
is either flat or the book's curve, linear between whole-year points and flat
beyond them.

\paragraph{Continuous time.} With protection paid when the trigger occurs and
premium paid continuously, both legs integrate the same $S(t)D(t)$:
\begin{equation}
s_{\mathrm{c}}=10^4\,\frac{(1-R)\int_0^T\lambda\,S(t)D(t)\,\mathrm{d}t}{\int_0^T S(t)D(t)\,\mathrm{d}t}
=10^4\,(1-R)\,\lambda,
\end{equation}
whatever the curve and tenor. This is the benchmark the document's \S8.3 refers to.

\paragraph{The quarterly scheme in closed form.} On a flat curve,
$S(t_{i-1})-S(t_i)=(e^{\lambda\Delta t}-1)S(t_i)$ and
$D(t_i-\Delta t/2)=e^{r\Delta t/2}D(t_i)$, so the sums cancel:
\begin{equation}
s=10^4\,(1-R)\,\frac{e^{\lambda\Delta t}-1}{\Delta t}\,e^{r\Delta t/2},
\qquad\frac{s}{s_{\mathrm{c}}}\approx1+\frac{(\lambda+r)\,\Delta t}{2}.
\end{equation}
The premium is paid in arrears with no accrual on a trigger, and the protection
half a period earlier; both raise the spread. The fallback of \S3.1.7 can never
apply: $A\ge\Delta t\,S(T)D(T)>0$ for any $p$ up to the cap.

\section{Benchmark Method}
% =========================================================================

\subsection{References}
The equations above are evaluated with \texttt{mpmath} at 50 significant digits,
implemented from the model document rather than the code. Wolfram evaluates the
same sums exactly, with the curve as a \texttt{Piecewise} function, from each
input's exact value at 60-digit precision, returning 30 digits.

\subsection{Cases}
Ten probabilities ($10^{-4}$ to 0.999), four tenors (1, 3, 5, 10 years), two
recoveries (0, 0.4) and two curves (flat 3\%, the book's
\texttt{DEFAULT\_YIELD\_CURVE}): \bmCases{} cases. The book trades $R=0$
(\S3.2); 0.4 checks the recovery term.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Benchmark, not validation.} The code is shown to compute the
  documented formula; whether a constant hazard rate and quarterly pricing suit
  the product is a separate question.
\item \textbf{Documentation.} \S8.3 and \S8.4 of the model document should be
  corrected to the figures above, and the code's docstring, which says the
  protection leg is discounted at $D(t_i)$, to the midpoint the code and the
  document both use.
\item \textbf{Basis legs.} Whether the gauge leg should be priced with Eq.~7, so
  that the basis measures the property against the gauge rather than one formula
  against another, is for the model owner.
\item \textbf{High probabilities.} Near the cap the spread exceeds $10^5$ bp; the
  formula is computed correctly there, but the number has no economic meaning.
\end{enumerate}
