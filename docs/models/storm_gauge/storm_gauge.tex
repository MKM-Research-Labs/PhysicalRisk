% 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
% ===================================================================
\mkmexecsummary

This document describes the Storm-Gauge Forward Model, which maps storm event
parameters---track geometry, intensity profile, and spatial footprint---to water
level time series at river gauge locations. The model is a core component of the
physical risk platform and serves as the bridge between synthetic storm generation
and downstream flood hazard estimation.

The forward model comprises three principal stages:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Spatial proximity computation} using the Haversine great-circle
          distance formula, with linear interpolation of the storm position along
          its track at each simulation time step.
    \item \textbf{Intensity attenuation} via a configurable spatial decay kernel
          (Gaussian, exponential, or linear) applied to the storm intensity field.
    \item \textbf{Intensity-to-level conversion} through a calibrated power-law
          transfer function that maps attenuated intensity to water level
          contributions at each gauge, incorporating response lag and exponential
          decay dynamics.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Key risk:} The model is used as an upstream component feeding the hazard
curve builder, PRS pricing engine, and portfolio flood model. Errors in the
spatial decay parameterisation or intensity-to-level calibration propagate directly
to flood probability estimates and, consequently, to swap pricing and mortgage
risk assessments.

The model is implemented in Python without external numerical dependencies beyond
the standard library \texttt{math} module, ensuring portability and auditability.

\mkmpurpose

\subsection{Purpose}
The Storm-Gauge Forward Model computes the water level response at each gauge
in a catchment given a parameterised storm event. It enables:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Generation of gauge-level flood time series for synthetic storm
          catalogues, supporting Monte Carlo hazard curve construction.
    \item Assessment of spatial correlation of flooding across multiple gauges
          within a catchment for portfolio-level risk aggregation.
    \item Sensitivity analysis of gauge responses to storm characteristics
          (intensity, track proximity, footprint width, decay kernel shape).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Scope}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{In scope:} Storm track interpolation; Haversine distance
          computation; spatial decay kernel application; intensity-to-level
          transfer function; response lag and decay dynamics; summary statistic
          extraction (peak level, exceedance durations, accumulation).
    \item \textbf{Out of scope:} Storm generation and track synthesis (handled
          by \texttt{storm\_factory.py}); hydrodynamic shallow water equation
          solvers; property-level depth--damage estimation; PRS pricing.
    \item \textbf{Upstream dependencies:} Storm objects with parameterised
          tracks (from \texttt{StormFactory} or portfolio random generators);
          gauge configurations from the gauge portfolio CDM.
    \item \textbf{Downstream consumers:} Gauge time series portfolio
          (\texttt{gaugets}); hazard curve builder; flood risk model; PRS
          pricing engine.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Model Classification}
Under the bank's model risk management framework, this model is classified as a
\emph{Tier~2 quantitative model}. It is deterministic given its inputs and does
not involve stochastic simulation internally, though its outputs feed into
stochastic ensemble analyses downstream.

\mkmframework

\subsection{Haversine Distance}
\label{sec:haversine}

The distance between a gauge at coordinates $(\phi_g, \lambda_g)$ and a storm
track point at $(\phi_s, \lambda_s)$ is computed using the Haversine formula on
a spherical Earth of radius $R = 6{,}371$\,km:

\begin{equation}
    a = \sin^2\!\left(\frac{\Delta\phi}{2}\right)
      + \cos\phi_g \,\cos\phi_s \,\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\Delta\lambda}{2}\right)
    \label{eq:haversine_a}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    d = 2R \,\arctan\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{1-a}}\right)
    \label{eq:haversine_d}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\Delta\phi = \phi_s - \phi_g$ and
$\Delta\lambda = \lambda_s - \lambda_g$ are the coordinate differences in
radians. The implementation uses \texttt{math.atan2} for numerical stability
near antipodal points, which is equivalent to $\arcsin(\sqrt{a})$ but avoids
domain issues when $a \approx 1$.

\subsection{Storm Track Interpolation}
\label{sec:track_interp}

A storm is represented by an ordered sequence of track points
$\{(\lambda_i, \phi_i, t_i, I_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$, where each point carries a
longitude, latitude, time (hours since storm start), and intensity on a
$[0, 100]$ scale.

At simulation time $t$, the model identifies the bracketing track points
$(p_{\text{prev}}, p_{\text{next}})$ such that
$t_{\text{prev}} \leq t < t_{\text{next}}$, and computes the interpolation
fraction:

\begin{equation}
    \alpha = \operatorname{clamp}\!\left(
        \frac{t - t_{\text{prev}}}{t_{\text{next}} - t_{\text{prev}}},\; 0,\; 1
    \right)
    \label{eq:interp_frac}
\end{equation}

The interpolated storm position and intensity are then:

\begin{align}
    \lambda_s(t) &= \lambda_{\text{prev}} + \alpha\,(\lambda_{\text{next}} - \lambda_{\text{prev}}) \label{eq:interp_lon} \\
    \phi_s(t) &= \phi_{\text{prev}} + \alpha\,(\phi_{\text{next}} - \phi_{\text{prev}}) \label{eq:interp_lat} \\
    I_s(t) &= I_{\text{prev}} + \alpha\,(I_{\text{next}} - I_{\text{prev}}) \label{eq:interp_intensity}
\end{align}

\noindent This is a piecewise linear interpolation along the track. For UK
catchment scales (typically $<$200\,km), the linear interpolation on geographic
coordinates introduces negligible error compared to great-circle interpolation.

\subsection{Spatial Decay Kernels}
\label{sec:decay_kernels}

The storm footprint is characterised by a footprint radius $F$ (in km) and a
decay kernel $k(\cdot)$ that attenuates intensity with distance from the storm
centre. The model normalises the gauge-to-storm distance by the footprint:

\begin{equation}
    \tilde{d} = \frac{d}{F}
    \label{eq:d_norm}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $d$ is the Haversine distance from
Equations~(\ref{eq:haversine_a})--(\ref{eq:haversine_d}). Three decay kernels
are supported:

\subsubsection{Gaussian Kernel}
\begin{equation}
    k_{\text{Gauss}}(\tilde{d}\,;\,\sigma) =
        \exp\!\left(-\frac{\tilde{d}^{\,2}}{2\sigma^2}\right)
    \label{eq:gaussian_kernel}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\sigma$ is the kernel bandwidth parameter (default 0.5). This
kernel provides smooth, bell-shaped attenuation and is the default choice. At
$\tilde{d} = \sigma$, the intensity has decayed to approximately 60.7\% of its
on-track value; at $\tilde{d} = 2\sigma$, it has decayed to approximately 13.5\%.

\subsubsection{Exponential Kernel}
\begin{equation}
    k_{\text{Exp}}(\tilde{d}\,;\,\lambda) =
        \exp\!\left(-\frac{\tilde{d}}{\lambda}\right)
    \label{eq:exponential_kernel}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\lambda$ is the characteristic decay length (default 0.5). This
kernel has a sharper peak at $\tilde{d}=0$ than the Gaussian and heavier tails,
making it suitable for storms with concentrated cores but extended rain bands.

\subsubsection{Linear Kernel}
\begin{equation}
    k_{\text{Lin}}(\tilde{d}\,;\,r) =
        \max\!\left(0,\; 1 - \frac{\tilde{d}}{r}\right)
    \label{eq:linear_kernel}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $r$ is the cut-off radius (default 0.5). This kernel provides a
compact support: intensity drops to zero at $\tilde{d} = r$ and remains zero
beyond. It is useful for modelling localised convective events with well-defined
boundaries.

\paragraph{Boundary condition.} For all kernels, $k(0) = 1$ by construction
(a gauge directly on the storm track receives full intensity), and
$k(\tilde{d}) \to 0$ as $\tilde{d} \to \infty$ (except for the linear kernel,
which reaches exactly zero at finite distance).

\subsection{Intensity at Gauge}
\label{sec:intensity_at_gauge}

Combining Sections~\ref{sec:haversine}--\ref{sec:decay_kernels}, the storm
intensity experienced at a gauge location at time $t$ is:

\begin{equation}
    I_{\text{gauge}}(t) = I_s(t) \times k\!\left(\tilde{d}(t)\right)
    \label{eq:intensity_at_gauge}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $I_s(t)$ is the interpolated storm intensity
(Equation~\ref{eq:interp_intensity}) and $\tilde{d}(t)$ is the normalised
distance from the gauge to the interpolated storm position at time $t$
(Equation~\ref{eq:d_norm}).

This product formulation assumes that storm intensity and spatial decay are
separable---a simplification that holds well for synoptic-scale extra-tropical
cyclones but may be less accurate for highly asymmetric mesoscale convective
systems.

\subsection{Intensity-to-Level Conversion}
\label{sec:intensity_to_level}

The transfer function from storm intensity to water level contribution uses a
calibrated power law. For a gauge with base level $L_{\text{base}}$, historical
high $L_{\text{high}}$ (or $1.1 \times L_{\text{severe}}$ if unavailable),
and sensitivity multiplier $s$:

\begin{equation}
    L_{\text{range}} = L_{\text{high}} - L_{\text{base}}
    \label{eq:level_range}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \Delta L(I) = \left(\frac{I}{100}\right)^{\!\gamma} \times L_{\text{range}} \times s
    \label{eq:power_law}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\gamma = 0.8$ is the power-law exponent. The sub-linear
exponent ($\gamma < 1$) ensures that moderate-intensity storms produce
proportionally larger water level responses than a linear mapping would imply,
reflecting the non-linear hydrological response of saturated catchments.

The calibration targets are:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item $I \approx 40$: water level approaches the flood alert threshold
    \item $I \approx 60$--$70$: water level approaches the flood warning threshold
    \item $I \approx 85+$: water level approaches the severe flood warning threshold
    \item $I = 100$: water level reaches the historical high
\end{itemize}

\noindent For $I \leq 0$, the contribution is identically zero.

\subsection{Temporal Response Dynamics}
\label{sec:temporal_dynamics}

The full gauge response incorporates a lag between storm passage and peak gauge
response, together with an asymmetric rise/fall dynamic. The water level at
time step $i$ is:

\begin{equation}
    L(t_i) = L_{\text{base}} + C(t_i)
    \label{eq:gauge_level}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $C(t_i)$ is the current water level contribution, updated via an
exponential smoothing scheme. Let $\Delta t$ denote the time resolution
(default 0.5\,hours), $\tau_{\text{lag}}$ the response lag (default 2.0\,hours),
and $\tau_{\text{decay}}$ the response decay time constant (default 12.0\,hours).
The lagged intensity is:

\begin{equation}
    I_{\text{lag}}(t_i) = I_{\text{gauge}}\!\left(t_{i - \lfloor \tau_{\text{lag}}/\Delta t \rfloor}\right)
    \label{eq:lagged_intensity}
\end{equation}

The target contribution at each step is $\Delta L(I_{\text{lag}})$ from
Equation~(\ref{eq:power_law}). The contribution is updated asymmetrically:

\begin{equation}
    C(t_i) = \begin{cases}
        C(t_{i-1}) + \bigl[\Delta L_{\text{target}} - C(t_{i-1})\bigr]
            \times \min\!\left(1,\; 3\,\dfrac{\Delta t}{\tau_{\text{decay}}}\right)
        & \text{if } \Delta L_{\text{target}} > C(t_{i-1}) \\[1em]
        C(t_{i-1}) + \bigl[\Delta L_{\text{target}} - C(t_{i-1})\bigr]
            \times \dfrac{\Delta t}{\tau_{\text{decay}}}
        & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:contribution_update}
\end{equation}

The rising limb uses a rate multiplier of~3 (i.e.\ the gauge rises three times
faster than it falls), which is consistent with observed hydrograph asymmetry in
UK river catchments. The falling limb follows a first-order exponential decay
towards the target level.

\subsection{Summary Statistics}
\label{sec:summary_stats}

The model extracts the following summary statistics from each simulated gauge
response:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Peak level} $L_{\text{peak}} = \max_i L(t_i)$ and its time of
          occurrence.
    \item \textbf{Peak exceedance} $E_{\text{peak}} = L_{\text{peak}} - L_{\text{alert}}$,
          which may be negative if the gauge does not flood.
    \item \textbf{Duration above thresholds}: the total time (in hours) that the
          water level exceeds each of the flood alert, flood warning, and severe
          flood warning thresholds:
          \begin{equation}
              D_{\theta} = \sum_{i:\, L(t_i) \geq \theta} \Delta t
              \label{eq:duration}
          \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Accumulation}: the time integral of exceedance above the flood
          alert level, measured in metre-hours:
          \begin{equation}
              A = \sum_{i} \max\!\bigl(0,\; L(t_i) - L_{\text{alert}}\bigr)\,\Delta t
              \label{eq:accumulation}
          \end{equation}
          This metric captures both the severity and duration of a flood event and
          is analogous to degree-days in climate analysis.
    \item \textbf{Peak intensity} $I_{\text{peak}} = \max_i I_{\text{gauge}}(t_i)$
          and its time of occurrence, which generally precedes the peak level by
          approximately $\tau_{\text{lag}}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Calibration Approach}

The power-law exponent $\gamma = 0.8$ was calibrated against Environment Agency
flood gauge records for Thames basin gauges, targeting the relationship between
storm intensity (as classified by Met Office severity categories) and observed
water level exceedances. The sub-linear exponent captures the non-linear
hydrological response whereby saturated catchments amplify moderate rainfall
events relative to a linear model.

The response lag $\tau_{\text{lag}} = 2.0$\,hours and decay time constant
$\tau_{\text{decay}} = 12.0$\,hours were calibrated against observed hydrograph
shapes from historical flood events in the Thames catchment. The asymmetric
rise/fall behaviour (factor of~3) is consistent with UK flood estimation
handbook guidance on typical hydrograph shapes for medium-sized catchments.

The gauge sensitivity parameter $s$ defaults to 1.0 but can be adjusted on a
per-gauge basis to account for local catchment characteristics (urbanisation,
soil type, antecedent conditions) that affect the rainfall--runoff response.

\mkmimplementation

\subsection{Software Architecture}

The model is implemented in the \texttt{src/models/stormgauge/} package,
comprising three modules:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \texttt{data\_structures.py} --- Enumerations (\texttt{DecayKernel},
          \texttt{IntensityProfile}) and dataclasses (\texttt{TrackPoint},
          \texttt{Storm}, \texttt{GaugeConfig}, \texttt{GaugeResponse}).
    \item \texttt{forward\_model.py} --- The \texttt{StormGaugeModel} class
          containing all computational methods.
    \item \texttt{storm\_factory.py} --- Helper functions for storm creation
          (\texttt{create\_storm}) and gauge loading
          (\texttt{load\_gauges\_from\_portfolio}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Computational Flow}

The primary entry point is \texttt{StormGaugeModel.compute\_response(storm, gauge)},
which executes the following steps:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item Generate a uniform time grid with spacing $\Delta t$:
          $t_i = i \cdot \Delta t$ for $i = 0, 1, \ldots, \lfloor T / \Delta t \rfloor$.
    \item For each time step, compute the storm intensity at the gauge location
          via \texttt{compute\_intensity\_at\_gauge()}, which performs track
          interpolation (Section~\ref{sec:track_interp}), Haversine distance
          calculation (Section~\ref{sec:haversine}), and decay kernel application
          (Section~\ref{sec:decay_kernels}).
    \item Apply the intensity-to-level transfer function with lag and exponential
          smoothing (Sections~\ref{sec:intensity_to_level}--\ref{sec:temporal_dynamics}).
    \item Extract summary statistics (Section~\ref{sec:summary_stats}).
    \item Return a \texttt{GaugeResponse} object containing both full time series
          and summary statistics.
\end{enumerate}

The batch method \texttt{compute\_all\_responses(storm, gauges)} iterates over
all gauges sequentially. No parallelisation is employed, as the per-gauge
computation is lightweight ($\mathcal{O}(N_t \cdot N_{\text{track}})$ per gauge,
where $N_t$ is the number of time steps and $N_{\text{track}}$ is the number of
track points).

\subsection{Numerical Considerations}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item The Haversine formula uses \texttt{math.atan2} rather than
          \texttt{math.asin} for numerical stability when $a \approx 1$.
    \item The interpolation fraction $\alpha$ is clamped to $[0, 1]$ to prevent
          extrapolation beyond the defined track.
    \item The exponential smoothing rate is clamped via $\min(1, \cdot)$ to
          prevent overshooting when the time step is large relative to the decay
          constant.
    \item All computations use 64-bit floating point arithmetic via Python's
          native \texttt{float} type.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dependencies}

The model depends solely on the Python standard library (\texttt{math},
\texttt{dataclasses}, \texttt{enum}, \texttt{typing}). No third-party numerical
libraries (NumPy, SciPy) are required, which simplifies deployment and auditing.

\subsection{Decay Kernel Selection}

The choice of decay kernel has a material impact on gauge responses for storms
that do not pass directly over the gauge. For a gauge at normalised distance
$\tilde{d} = 1.0$ from the storm track (i.e.\ one footprint radius away):

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Decay factor at $\tilde{d} = 1.0$ for each kernel (default parameters)}
    \label{tab:decay_comparison}
    \begin{tabular}{lcc}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Kernel} & \textbf{$k(1.0)$} & \textbf{Relative intensity} \\
        \midrule
        Gaussian ($\sigma = 0.5$) & $\exp(-2) \approx 0.135$ & 13.5\% \\
        Exponential ($\lambda = 0.5$) & $\exp(-2) \approx 0.135$ & 13.5\% \\
        Linear ($r = 0.5$) & $\max(0, 1-2) = 0.0$ & 0.0\% \\

\noindent The Gaussian and exponential kernels coincide at this distance for

to the cut-off parameter.

\subsection{Power-Law Exponent}

The power-law exponent $\gamma$ controls the non-linearity of the
intensity-to-level mapping. A sensitivity analysis varying $\gamma$ over the
range $[0.5, 1.5]$ for a fixed intensity of $I = 50$ yields:

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering

\noindent The model is moderately sensitive to $\gamma$: a $\pm 25\%$ change in

mid-range intensities.

\subsection{Response Lag and Decay}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Increasing $\tau_{\text{lag}}$ delays the peak level relative to peak
          intensity. This primarily affects the timing of threshold crossings
          but has minimal impact on peak magnitude.
    \item Increasing $\tau_{\text{decay}}$ extends the duration above thresholds
          and increases the accumulation metric, without significantly affecting
          peak level. Doubling $\tau_{\text{decay}}$ from 12 to 24 hours
          approximately doubles the duration above alert for a typical event.
    \item The rise rate multiplier (3$\times$) ensures that the gauge responds
          rapidly to increasing storm intensity. Reducing this to 1$\times$ would
          result in a symmetric hydrograph and underestimate peak levels for
          rapidly intensifying storms.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Footprint Radius}

The footprint radius $F$ scales the effective reach of the storm. For fixed
decay parameters, doubling $F$ doubles the physical distance at which a given
normalised decay level is reached. This parameter has a first-order effect on
which gauges in a catchment are affected by a given storm and is the primary
driver of spatial correlation in portfolio flood risk.

\mkmvalidation

\subsection{Unit Testing}

The model has comprehensive unit test coverage (included in the project test
suite under \texttt{tests/}). Tests verify:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Haversine distance against known geodetic benchmarks.
    \item Decay kernel boundary conditions: $k(0) = 1$ for all kernels;
          $k(\tilde{d}) = 0$ for the linear kernel when $\tilde{d} \geq r$.
    \item Intensity-to-level monotonicity: higher intensity always produces
          higher water level contribution.
    \item Response lag correctness: peak level occurs after peak intensity by
          approximately $\tau_{\text{lag}}$.
    \item Conservation: a zero-intensity storm produces no water level change.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Backtesting Against Historical Events}

The model has been backtested against Environment Agency gauge records for
selected Thames basin gauges during historical storm events. Key findings:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Peak level errors are typically within $\pm 0.3$\,m for events below
          the severe warning threshold.
    \item The model tends to underestimate peak levels for events with significant
          antecedent rainfall (saturated catchment conditions), as it does not
          model soil moisture state.
    \item Timing of peak level is generally within $\pm 1$\,hour of observed,
          with the Gaussian decay kernel providing the best temporal profile match.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cross-Validation with Downstream Models}

The gauge responses produced by this model are consumed by the hazard curve
builder and PRS pricing engine. Validation checks confirm that:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item The distribution of peak levels across a synthetic storm catalogue is
          consistent with GEV fits to historical annual maxima.
    \item Spatial correlation of flooding across gauges is qualitatively
          consistent with observed patterns for the Thames catchment.
\end{itemize}

% ===================================================================
% 8. MODEL LIMITATIONS AND KNOWN WEAKNESSES
% ===================================================================
\mkmlimitations

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{No antecedent conditions.} The model does not account for soil
          moisture, groundwater levels, or preceding rainfall. A storm of given
          intensity always produces the same gauge response regardless of prior
          conditions. This is a significant simplification for multi-event
          sequences.

    \item \textbf{Linear track interpolation.} Storm positions are interpolated
          linearly between track points. This may introduce errors for storms
          with curved tracks, though the effect is small for densely sampled
          tracks (the default is 25 points).

    \item \textbf{Separable intensity and decay.} The factored form
          $I_{\text{gauge}} = I_s \times k(\tilde{d})$ assumes that the spatial
          footprint shape does not vary with intensity. In reality, stronger
          storms may have broader or narrower footprints.

    \item \textbf{Static sensitivity.} The gauge sensitivity parameter $s$ is
          constant and does not vary with water level or season. In practice,
          gauges in urbanised areas may exhibit non-linear responses due to
          drainage infrastructure capacity limits.

    \item \textbf{Spherical Earth approximation.} The Haversine formula assumes
          a perfect sphere of radius 6,371\,km. For UK catchment scales, the
          ellipsoidal correction is negligible ($<$0.3\% error), but this would
          need revision for continental-scale applications.

    \item \textbf{Fixed asymmetric response.} The rise rate multiplier of~3 is
          hard-coded and not gauge-specific. Different catchment morphologies
          (steep vs.\ flat, urban vs.\ rural) may warrant different asymmetry
          ratios.

    \item \textbf{No tidal interaction.} The model does not account for tidal
          influences on water levels at estuarine or coastal gauges. For tidal
          reaches of the Thames, this omission may lead to underestimation of
          compound flood risk.

    \item \textbf{Single-footprint model.} Each storm has a single footprint
          radius. Real storms may exhibit multiple precipitation bands with
          different widths and intensities.
\end{enumerate}

% 9. GOVERNANCE VALIDATION QUESTIONS
% ===================================================================
\mkmgovernance

\mkmvqitem{1}{Ronseal Test}{Does the model do what it says on the tin?}
Yes. The model maps storm event parameters (track geometry, intensity profile, footprint) to water level time series at each gauge location. It delivers: (i)~spatial proximity computation via Haversine distance, (ii)~three configurable decay kernels (Gaussian, exponential, linear), (iii)~calibrated power-law intensity-to-level conversion with lag and decay dynamics, and (iv)~summary statistics extraction (peak level, exceedance durations, accumulation). All documented outputs are produced and consumed downstream.

\mkmvqitem{2}{Accuracy and Stability}{Does the model provide timely, accurate and stable results?}
The model is fully deterministic: identical storm and gauge inputs produce identical water level time series. Peak level errors are within $\pm$0.3\,m for events below the severe warning threshold when backtested against EA gauge records. Peak timing is accurate to $\pm$1 hour. The Gaussian decay kernel provides the best temporal profile match against historical events. Cross-validation confirms consistency between gauge responses and GEV fits to historical annual maxima.

\mkmvqitem{3}{Resource Usage}{Does the model consume excessive computational resources?}
No. The model is implemented using only the Python standard library (\texttt{math} module). Per-gauge computation is $O(N_t \times N_{\text{track}})$ where $N_t$ is the number of time steps and $N_{\text{track}}$ is the number of track points. A typical storm response (48 hours, $\Delta t = 0.5\,$h, 25 track points) completes in under 10\,ms per gauge. Full catchment processing (25 gauges) completes in under 250\,ms. Memory usage is negligible.

\mkmvqitem{4}{Override Controls}{Are there controls over manual overrides?}
The model exposes a per-gauge sensitivity parameter $s$ (default 1.0) that scales the intensity-to-level transfer function. This parameter is set in the gauge configuration data and is not modifiable at runtime. The power-law exponent ($\gamma = 0.8$), response lag ($\tau_{\text{lag}} = 2.0$\,h), and decay time constant ($\tau_{\text{decay}} = 12.0$\,h) are hard-coded constants. All changes require code modification with version control tracking.

\mkmvqitem{5}{Output Consumption}{How is the output consumed for risk decision-making?}
Gauge response time series are stored in the \texttt{gaugets/} directory as per-gauge JSON files. These feed directly into: (a)~the GEV Hazard Curve Model for annual maxima extraction and flood frequency estimation, (b)~the Flood Risk Model for property-level depth calculation, and (c)~gauge-level risk reports for MRC review. Summary statistics (peak level, durations above thresholds) are displayed in the interactive governance dashboard.

\mkmvqitem{6}{Model Chaining}{Is the model's output used as input to another model?}
Yes. The model is a core mid-chain component. Direct downstream consumers are: (i)~GEV Hazard Curve Model (MKM-HAZARD-001), which uses gauge response distributions; (ii)~Flood Risk Model (MKM-FLOOD-001), which uses water level time series; and (iii)~Timeseries Statistics Engine (MKM-TS-001), which computes descriptive statistics from gauge records. Upstream, the model consumes storm objects from the Storm Intensity Distribution (MKM-STORM-001) and gauge configurations from the portfolio CDM.

\mkmvqitem{7}{Weaknesses}{Does the model have weaknesses and limitations of use?}
Yes; eight limitations are documented in Section~8. The most material are: (i)~no antecedent soil moisture conditioning; (ii)~separable intensity--decay assumption; (iii)~static gauge sensitivity parameter; (iv)~no tidal interaction at estuarine gauges; and (v)~single-footprint model (no multi-band storms). The linear track interpolation introduces negligible error for densely sampled tracks.

\mkmvqitem{8}{Monitoring}{What type of monitoring is required?}
Monitoring should include: (a)~annual comparison of simulated peak levels against EA gauge records for recent flood events; (b)~review of the power-law exponent calibration against any newly available hydrological data; (c)~decay kernel selection review if new gauge locations are added outside the Thames catchment; (d)~per-gauge sensitivity parameter review against observed rainfall--runoff relationships; (e)~cross-validation of spatial correlation patterns against observed multi-gauge flood events.

\mkmvqitem{9}{Overall Risk}{Is the overall model risk acceptable?}
Acceptable. As a Tier~2 model, the Storm-Gauge Forward Model is a material input to downstream Tier~1 models but does not directly produce regulated outputs. The dependency-free implementation ensures portability and auditability. The primary risk---omission of antecedent conditions---is mitigated by the conservative rise rate multiplier (3$\times$) and can be addressed through a planned soil moisture deficit extension. Per-gauge sensitivity calibration from historical data is recommended as a near-term improvement.
