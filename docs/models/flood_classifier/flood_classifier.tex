% 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
% ===================================================================
\mkmexecsummary

This document presents the mathematical framework, implementation, and validation of
the Flood Probability Classifier (MKM-FC-001), a Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) model
that estimates per-gauge flood probability as a function of log-transformed water level,
storm time, and hydrograph derivatives:
$F\!\left(\ln\tfrac{w}{s},\, \ln\tfrac{t{+}1}{T},\, \Delta\ln\tfrac{w}{s},\,
\Delta^2\ln\tfrac{w}{s}\right) \to P(\text{flood})$,
where $s$ is the severe flood threshold and $T = 168$ hours.

The classifier is trained per gauge using stress test vectors derived from historical
storm simulations. All features are log-transformed relative to gauge-specific
thresholds, producing a scale-invariant representation that generalises across gauges
with different flood severity levels.

\textbf{v1.3 enhancement} introduced four log-transformed features:
$\ln(w/s)$, $\ln((t{+}1)/168)$, $\Delta\ln(w/s)$, and $\Delta^2\ln(w/s)$.
This produced a mean AUC of 0.9525 across 40 gauges, a 3.2\% improvement over v1.2.

\textbf{v2.0 upgrade (multi-storm):} Version~2.0 replaces the single-storm
training corpus with sequences generated by the Storm Sequence Generator (MKM-SS-001).
Instead of 1{,}000 independent storms ($\times$168h = 168{,}000 vectors per gauge),
the classifier now trains on \textbf{10{,}000 multi-storm sequences}
($\times$168h = \textbf{1{,}680{,}000 vectors per gauge})---ten times the data.
Crucially, multi-storm hydrographs contain \emph{multi-peak} shapes driven by
compounding antecedent-wetness effects that single-storm data cannot represent.
The feature space and hyperparameters are unchanged from v1.3; the gains come
entirely from richer, larger training data.

The mean AUC-ROC across all 52 Thames gauges improved from 0.9525 (v1.3) to
\textbf{$\approx$0.993} (v2.0), a further 4.2\% improvement.
The stressm classifiers are stored in \texttt{data/output/stressm/}
and trained via \texttt{phys.py port --stressm --train-classifier}.

\textbf{Key risk:} The model is trained on synthetically noised data (time-decaying
Gaussian perturbation), which creates intentional early-hour uncertainty. The derivative
features are computed from the log-transformed noised hydrograph, preserving the
uncertainty structure in the velocity and acceleration signals.

% 2. MODEL PURPOSE AND SCOPE
% ===================================================================
\mkmpurpose

\subsection{Purpose}
The Flood Probability Classifier serves two functions within the platform:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Stress testing:} Given a stress storm scenario, estimate the
          probability of flood alert breach at each gauge as a function of
          evolving water levels during the storm event.
    \item \textbf{P\&L estimation:} Convert flood probabilities into cash-equivalent
          stress P\&L for PRS trade positions, using the CDS-in-stress analogy:
          $\text{cash\_price} = \text{notional} \times P(\text{flood}) \times \text{direction}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Scope}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{In scope:} Per-gauge binary classification (flood/no-flood) using
          water level, temporal, and hydrograph derivative features.
    \item \textbf{Out of scope:} Multi-gauge spatial correlation, flood severity
          estimation (depth/velocity), and climate change projection.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Model Chain Position}
\label{sec:model_chain}
The classifier now sits downstream of the Storm Sequence Generator:
\[
\text{MKM-SI-001} \to
\textbf{MKM-SS-001}\ (\text{sequences})
\to \text{MKM-SG-001}\ (\text{hydrograph})
\to \textbf{MKM-FC-001}\ (\text{v2.0})
\to \text{Stress P\&L}
\]
For single-storm stress testing (Trading Desk Tab~7), the v1.3 classifiers stored in
\texttt{data/output/stress/} remain in use. The v2.0 stressm classifiers stored in
\texttt{data/output/stressm/} power the Portfolio Stress tab (Tab~8).

% 3. MATHEMATICAL FRAMEWORK
% ===================================================================
\mkmframework

\subsection{Model Family}
The model uses \texttt{sklearn.ensemble.GradientBoostingClassifier}, which constructs
an additive ensemble of regression trees optimised under the binomial deviance loss:

\[
\mathcal{L}(y, F) = -\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left[ y_i \log p_i + (1 - y_i) \log(1 - p_i) \right]
\]

where $p_i = \sigma(F(x_i))$ and $\sigma$ is the logistic function. The model is built
sequentially:

\[
F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \nu \cdot h_m(x)
\]

where $h_m$ is a regression tree fit to the negative gradient of the loss, and $\nu$
is the learning rate.

\subsection{Feature Space}
The model uses a four-dimensional log-transformed feature space:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llp{7.5cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Feature} & \textbf{Type} & \textbf{Description} \\
\midrule
\texttt{log\_h\_s} & Continuous &
    $\ln\!\left(\tilde{w}(t) / s\right)$; water level relative to severe threshold $s$.
    Zero at severe level, negative below, positive above. \\
\texttt{log\_t\_end} & Continuous &
    $\ln\!\left((t{+}1) / 168\right)$; log storm time.
    Ranges from $\approx{-5.1}$ (hour 0) to $0$ (hour 167). \\
\texttt{delta\_log\_h} & Continuous &
    $\Delta\ln(w/s)(t) = \texttt{log\_h\_s}(t) - \texttt{log\_h\_s}(t{-}1)$;
    log velocity (positive rising, negative falling). \\
\texttt{delta2\_log\_h} & Continuous &
    $\Delta^2\ln(w/s)(t) = \texttt{delta\_log\_h}(t) - \texttt{delta\_log\_h}(t{-}1)$;
    log acceleration. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent All derivative features are computed from the log-transformed noised
hydrograph $\ln(\tilde{w}(t)/s)$, not the clean signal. This ensures the model
learns to interpret derivative signals that include the same observation noise present
in real-time monitoring. At prediction time, raw derivatives $\Delta w$ and
$\Delta^2 w$ are passed in and converted to log-space internally by the predictor.

\subsection{Physical Motivation for Log Transformation}
The raw-level feature space has two limitations:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Gauge heterogeneity:} Thames gauges have severe thresholds ranging
          from approximately 3\,m to 7\,m AOD. A water level of 4.5\,m carries very
          different flood implications depending on the gauge's threshold. The log
          transform $\ln(w/s)$ normalises to a common scale: $\ln(w/s)=0$ always
          means ``at severe threshold'', regardless of absolute level.
    \item \textbf{Velocity interpretation:} A rise of 0.3\,m/h from 1\,m is alarming;
          the same rise from 5\,m is less informative. Log-velocity
          $\Delta\ln(w/s) \approx \Delta w / w$ normalises by the current level,
          providing a relative rate of change. This is analogous to using log-returns
          in financial risk models.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The time feature $\ln((t{+}1)/168)$ is restored from v1.2 in log scale.
This compresses the early storm hours (where uncertainty is highest) and stretches the
late hours (where remaining uncertainty is lower), matching the structure of the
noise model $\sigma(t)$.

\subsection{Rising vs Falling Water}
The log-velocity feature $\Delta\ln(w/s)$ preserves the directional asymmetry
introduced in v1.2:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Rising water} ($\Delta\ln(w/s) > 0$): The storm has not yet peaked.
          $P(\text{flood})$ should reflect uncertainty about the eventual peak.
    \item \textbf{Falling water} ($\Delta\ln(w/s) < 0$): The storm has already peaked.
          If near alert level, the peak was likely above it.
          $P(\text{flood})$ should be higher, reflecting stronger evidence.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The log-acceleration $\Delta^2\ln(w/s)$ further discriminates between a
decelerating rise (approaching peak) and an accelerating rise (early surge).

\subsection{Target Variable}
Binary label $y \in \{0, 1\}$ indicating whether the storm breaches the gauge's
flood alert trigger level at any point during the event. The label is computed from
the stress test vector:

\[
y_i = \begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } \max_{t} w_i(t) \geq \text{alert\_level} \\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]

\subsection{Training Data}

\subsubsection{v2.0 (stressm): Multi-Storm Sequence Training}

Version~2.0 trains on the full catalogue of 10{,}000 \texttt{StormSequence} objects
generated by MKM-SS-001 (Section~\ref{sec:model_chain}). Each sequence contains 1--5
individual storms with calibrated durations, inter-storm gaps, and correlated intensity
factors. The training corpus is therefore the complete population, not a balanced sample.

For each sequence, a multi-peak hydrograph is synthesised at each gauge by compositing
the individual storm responses in temporal order. Each storm contributes a water level
arc derived from its intensity factor and the gauge's spatial response parameters
(\texttt{base\_level\_m}, \texttt{level\_change\_m}, \texttt{peak\_position}).
Between storms, the water level decays toward baseline at the catchment drainage rate,
modelling the partial recovery that determines antecedent wetness for the next storm.

The same time-decaying Gaussian noise is applied per storm arc:
\[
\tilde{w}(t) = w(t) + \varepsilon(t), \quad
\varepsilon(t) \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0, \sigma(t)^2\right)
\]
\[
\sigma(t) = 0.5 \cdot e^{-3t / 167} + 0.1
\]

The feature extraction is identical to v1.3; the features are computed from the full
168-hour noised multi-peak hydrograph:
\[
\mathbf{x}_i = \left[\, \ell(t),\; \tau(t),\; \Delta\ell(t),\; \Delta^2\ell(t) \,\right]
\in \mathbb{R}^4
\]

\noindent Total training corpus: \textbf{1{,}680{,}000 vectors per gauge}
(10{,}000 sequences $\times$ 168 hours).

\subsubsection{v1.3 (stress): Single-Storm Balanced Training}

For reference, the v1.3 (single-storm) training pipeline used a balanced sample of
1{,}000 storms from the 10{,}000-storm catalogue:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Alert storms (260):} All storms that breached the flood alert trigger,
          sourced from \texttt{stress\_storms.json}.
    \item \textbf{Non-alert storms (740):} Randomly sampled from the non-alert population.
\end{itemize}

Total: 168{,}000 vectors per gauge (1{,}000 storms $\times$ 168 hours).

\textbf{Why v2.0 does not need balanced sampling:} The multi-storm sequences naturally
contain diverse hydrograph shapes including: (i)~sequences where the gauge barely rises
(Storm~1 is weak, far away); (ii)~sequences where Storm~1 causes a modest rise and
Storm~2 produces the flood (antecedent-wetness amplification); (iii)~catastrophic
sequences where all storms breach the severe threshold. This diversity provides the
classifier with sufficient negative examples without artificial sub-sampling.

Log-level features are computed identically to v1.3:
\[
\ell(t) = \ln\!\left(\max\!\left(\frac{\tilde{w}(t)}{s},\; 10^{-8}\right)\right),
\quad
\Delta\ell(t) = \ell(t) - \ell(t{-}1), \quad
\Delta^2\ell(t) = \Delta\ell(t) - \Delta\ell(t{-}1)
\]
\[
\tau(t) = \ln\!\left(\frac{t + 1}{168}\right)
\]
with boundary conditions $\Delta\ell(0) = \Delta^2\ell(0) = 0$.

\section{Implementation Details}

\subsection{Source Code}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Primary module:} \texttt{src/models/stress/flood\_classifier.py}
    \item \textbf{Training class:} \texttt{FloodClassifierTrainer}
    \item \textbf{Prediction class:} \texttt{FloodPredictor}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Training Pipelines}

\subsubsection{v2.0 stressm Pipeline}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item Generate 10{,}000 StormSequence objects via MKM-SS-001 (\texttt{--stressm})
    \item For each gauge, synthesise 168-hour multi-peak hydrograph per sequence
    \item Apply time-decaying noise; compute 4 log-transformed feature vectors
    \item Build feature matrix $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{1{,}680{,}000 \times 4}$
          and label vector $\mathbf{y} \in \{0,1\}^{1{,}680{,}000}$
    \item Skip single-class gauges
    \item Stratified 80/20 train-test split with \texttt{random\_state=42}
    \item Fit \texttt{GradientBoostingClassifier} on training set
    \item Evaluate on test set: accuracy, AUC-ROC, Brier score, log loss
    \item Serialise model to \texttt{data/output/stressm/GAUGE-\{id\}.joblib}
    \item Write summary to \texttt{data/output/stressm/training\_summary.json}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{v1.3 stress Pipeline (retained for single-storm Tab~7)}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item Load stress test vectors from \texttt{data/input/thames/stress\_vectors.json}
    \item For each gauge, extract feature matrix $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{168{,}000 \times 4}$
          and label vector $\mathbf{y} \in \{0,1\}^{168{,}000}$
    \item Stratified 80/20 split; fit; evaluate; serialise to
          \texttt{data/output/stress/GAUGE-\{id\}.joblib}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Prediction Interface}
Three prediction modes are provided:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \texttt{predict(gauge\_id, water\_level, hour, delta\_w, delta2\_w)}
          --- Single-point $\hat{p}$; \texttt{delta\_w} and \texttt{delta2\_w}
          default to 0.0 (steady state) for backward compatibility
    \item \texttt{predict\_series(gauge\_id, levels, hours, delta\_ws, delta2\_ws)}
          --- Vectorised time series
    \item \texttt{predict\_surface(gauge\_id, ..., delta\_w, delta2\_w)}
          --- Full $(w, t)$ probability grid at constant derivatives (default 0.0)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Probability Surface}
The \texttt{predict\_surface} mode produces a two-dimensional grid of flood
probabilities $P(w, t)$ over a range of water levels $w$ and storm hours $t$.
This is exposed in the stress test UI as the ``Surface'' tab, displaying a
heat-map table with the following structure:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
\textbf{Axis} & \textbf{Description} \\
\midrule
Rows (Y) & Water levels in 0.5\,m steps, descending from highest to lowest \\
Columns (X) & Storm hours at 4-hour intervals: H0, H4, H8, \ldots, H56 \\
Cells & $P(\text{flood})$ as a percentage to one decimal place \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The surface grid is computed on-demand during stress scenario execution with
$\Delta w = 0$ and $\Delta^2 w = 0$ (steady-state assumption), representing the
probability at each grid point if the water level were neither rising nor falling.
The hourly time series uses actual derivatives computed from the hydrograph. Water
level bounds are derived from the storm hydrograph range, extended to cover the
gauge's full trigger band (alert through severe + 0.5\,m buffer).

\textbf{Visual encoding:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Cells below the alert level: neutral (white) background with grey
          intensity shading proportional to $P(\text{flood})$.
    \item Cells in the alert band ($\text{alert} \leq w < \text{warning}$):
          amber background (\texttt{\#FFF8E1}).
    \item Cells in the warning band ($\text{warning} \leq w < \text{severe}$):
          orange background (\texttt{\#FFF3E0}).
    \item Cells at or above the severe level: red background (\texttt{\#FFEBEE}).
    \item Post knock-out (after the first trigger breach hour): cells are left
          blank with a grey background, reflecting contract termination.
\end{itemize}

Within each trigger band, cell opacity increases proportionally to the probability
value, creating a heat-map effect that highlights regions of high flood risk.

\subsection{Serialisation}
Models are persisted using \texttt{joblib.dump()} in the standard sklearn format.
Each gauge model is stored independently at \texttt{GAUGE-\{id\}.joblib}, enabling
selective retraining without full pipeline re-execution.

\subsubsection{Asymmetry Evidence: Rising vs Falling Water}
The key diagnostic is the model's ability to distinguish rising from falling water
at the same level. The following table shows $P(\text{flood})$ for gauge
\texttt{GAUGE-32e88723} (AUC\,=\,0.957, flood rate\,=\,10.0\%) at hour\,=\,20
with $|\Delta w| = 0.4$\,m/h:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rcccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Level (m)} & \textbf{Rising} & \textbf{Steady} & \textbf{Falling}
    & \textbf{$\Delta P$} \\
\midrule
3.3 (sub-alert) & 0.003 & 0.027 & 0.103 & $-$0.099 \\
3.8 & 0.033 & 0.140 & 0.462 & $-$0.429 \\
4.3 (near alert) & 0.261 & 0.646 & 0.882 & $-$0.622 \\
4.8 (above alert) & 0.831 & 0.942 & 0.976 & $-$0.144 \\
5.3 & 0.984 & 0.992 & 0.996 & $-$0.012 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The model has learned the physically correct signal:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item At sub-alert levels, \textbf{falling water implies higher $P(\text{flood})$}:
          if the water is at 4.3\,m and falling, the storm already peaked higher,
          providing strong evidence that alert was breached.
    \item At sub-alert levels, \textbf{rising water implies lower $P(\text{flood})$}:
          the storm has not yet peaked, so there is genuine uncertainty about
          whether the peak will reach the alert level.
    \item Above the alert level, all states converge to $P \approx 1$ regardless
          of direction, as the evidence for flood breach is overwhelming.
\end{itemize}

The maximum asymmetry occurs near the alert threshold (4.3\,m), where the directional
signal is most informative: $\Delta P = -0.622$. The v1.1 model would output the
``Steady'' column for both rising and falling water.

% 8. LIMITATIONS
% ===================================================================
\mkmlimitations

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Hydrograph-derived features only:} The four features (water level,
          hour, velocity, acceleration) are all derived from the local hydrograph.
          External features (rainfall intensity, upstream levels, antecedent
          soil moisture) could further improve discrimination but would require
          expanded data pipelines.
    \item \textbf{No spatial correlation:} Each gauge is modelled independently.
          Correlated flood events across multiple gauges are not captured.
    \item \textbf{Training noise regime:} The time-decaying noise is a modelling
          choice, not an empirical calibration. Different noise functions would
          yield different AUC profiles.
    \item \textbf{Binary classification only:} The model predicts flood/no-flood.
          Flood severity (depth, velocity, duration) is not estimated.
    \item \textbf{Stationarity assumed:} The classifier does not account for
          changing flood characteristics due to climate change or infrastructure
          modifications.
    \item \textbf{Alert-level threshold:} Training uses the FloodAlert trigger
          level. Models trained on Warning or Severe thresholds would produce
          different classifiers.
\end{enumerate}
