% ===================================================================
% 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
% ===================================================================
\mkmexecsummary

The Physical Risk Swap (PRS) is a credit-derivative-like instrument that transfers
physical flood risk from a protection buyer (e.g.\ a mortgage lender or property owner)
to a protection seller (e.g.\ a reinsurer or capital markets investor). The PRS pays out
upon the occurrence of a defined flood trigger event at a reference gauge or property
location, in exchange for a periodic premium (the ``spread'').

This document describes the analytical pricing model implemented in the MKM PhysicalRisk
platform. The model computes fair PRS spreads using a CDS-equivalent quarterly pricing
framework, where the annual flood hazard rate derived from the upstream GEV hazard curve
model is converted to a continuous-time Poisson intensity. Fair spreads are calculated as
the ratio of the protection leg present value to the risky annuity, expressed in basis
points.

Property-level PRS pricing is derived from full flood simulation rather than a
parametric formula. The gauge flood signal is propagated to each property via an
exponential retention model that attenuates the water surface elevation with distance.
The property floods only when the propagated water level exceeds its elevation plus
floor level. The resulting property-level hazard rate is fitted via GEV and priced
through the same CDS framework. The basis (gauge spread minus property spread) is
therefore an emergent quantity driven by three physical factors:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Distance}: exponential attenuation of the flood signal ($e^{-d/L}$,
          $L = 3{,}000$\,m),
    \item \textbf{Elevation}: the property's height above the gauge raises the flood
          threshold, and
    \item \textbf{Terrain}: the Environment Agency flood zone classification of the
          property location, captured implicitly through the flood simulation boundary
          conditions.
\end{enumerate}

Spread decomposition into distance and elevation components is achieved via two
synthetic simulation horizons (SHD and SHE), enabling attribution of the total basis
to its physical drivers.

The model supports two pricing paths: a lightweight analytical implementation with no
external dependencies, and an alternative QuantLib-based implementation using the ISDA
CDS engine for cross-validation. Both paths produce consistent results to within
rounding tolerance for flat hazard rate inputs.

\textbf{Key risk:} The model assumes a constant (flat) hazard rate over the contract
tenor. Time-varying hazard rates arising from climate change trends are not captured.
The spread decomposition is path-dependent (distance-first vs elevation-first ordering
yields different component magnitudes).

\mkmpurpose

\subsection{Purpose}

The PRS pricing model serves three primary functions within the PhysicalRisk platform:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Gauge-level PRS pricing}: Compute the fair annual spread (in basis
          points) for a PRS contract referencing a specific river gauge, given the
          gauge's annual flood hazard rate, a chosen trigger level, and contract
          parameters (tenor, recovery rate, risk-free rate).
    \item \textbf{Property-level PRS pricing}: Compute property-level PRS spreads from
          simulated property flood hazard rates, where the basis versus the gauge
          emerges from physical flood propagation (distance attenuation and elevation
          thresholds).
    \item \textbf{Basis analysis}: Decompose the gauge--property spread difference into
          distance and elevation components via synthetic simulation horizons (SHD/SHE)
          for risk management and hedging purposes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Scope}

The model prices single-name PRS contracts with the following characteristics:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Quarterly premium payments (CDS-standard schedule).
    \item Single trigger event (flood occurrence at a defined severity level).
    \item Zero recovery rate (100\% loss given default).
    \item Flat risk-free discount curve.
    \item Tenors of 1 to 30 years (typically 5 years).
\end{itemize}

The model does not price:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Portfolio or index PRS tranches,
    \item Contracts with variable or stochastic recovery,
    \item Contracts with counterparty credit risk adjustment (CVA/DVA),
    \item Contracts with non-standard payment frequencies.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Trigger Levels}

Three trigger levels are supported, corresponding to Environment Agency flood warning
severity classifications:

% 3. MATHEMATICAL FRAMEWORK
% ===================================================================
\mkmframework

\subsection{Analytical Pricing (prs\_analytical.py)}
\label{sec:analytical}

The analytical pricing model follows a standard CDS pricing framework adapted for
physical flood risk. The key insight is that flood occurrence can be modelled as a
Poisson point process, where the survival function takes the same exponential form as
in reduced-form credit models.

\subsubsection{Hazard Rate Conversion}

The upstream GEV hazard curve model provides an annual flood probability
$\lambda_{\text{annual}} \in (0,1)$, representing the probability of at least one flood
event exceeding the trigger threshold in any given year. This is converted to a
continuous-time Poisson intensity $\lambda$ via:

\begin{equation}
    \lambda = -\ln\!\big(1 - \lambda_{\text{annual}}\big)
    \label{eq:hazard_conversion}
\end{equation}

This transformation ensures consistency between the discrete annual probability and the
continuous-time survival function. For small $\lambda_{\text{annual}}$, the approximation
$\lambda \approx \lambda_{\text{annual}}$ holds, but the exact transformation is used
throughout.

To prevent numerical overflow, $\lambda_{\text{annual}}$ is capped at 0.999 before
conversion.

\subsubsection{Survival and Discount Functions}

Under the constant hazard rate assumption, the survival probability at time $t$ is:

\begin{equation}
    S(t) = \exp(-\lambda\, t)
    \label{eq:survival}
\end{equation}

The risk-free discount factor at time $t$ under continuous compounding is:

\begin{equation}
    D(t) = \exp(-r\, t)
    \label{eq:discount}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $r$ is the risk-free rate (default $r = 0.03$).

\subsubsection{Quarterly Discretisation}

The contract is discretised into quarterly periods of length $\Delta t = 0.25$ years.
For a contract of tenor $T$ years, the number of periods is $n = 4T$, and the $i$-th
payment date is $t_i = i \cdot \Delta t$ for $i = 1, \ldots, n$.

\subsubsection{Risky Annuity (Premium Leg)}

The risky annuity represents the expected present value of receiving 1\,bp per annum,
paid quarterly, conditional on no trigger event having occurred:

\begin{equation}
    A = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Delta t \cdot S(t_i) \cdot D(t_i)
    \label{eq:risky_annuity}
\end{equation}

This is the denominator in the fair spread calculation and represents the ``price''
of the premium leg per unit of spread.

\subsubsection{Protection Leg}

The protection leg represents the expected present value of receiving $(1 - R)$ upon
a trigger event, where $R$ is the recovery rate. The probability of a trigger event
occurring in the interval $(t_{i-1}, t_i]$ is $S(t_{i-1}) - S(t_i)$, and the payment
is assumed to occur at the midpoint of the interval:

\begin{equation}
    P = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (1 - R) \cdot \big[S(t_{i-1}) - S(t_i)\big] \cdot D\!\left(t_i - \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\right)
    \label{eq:protection_leg}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $t_0 = 0$ and $S(t_0) = 1$.

\subsubsection{Fair Spread}

The fair spread is the premium rate $s^*$ that equates the present value of the premium
leg and the protection leg:

\begin{equation}
    s^* \cdot A = P \quad \implies \quad s^* = \frac{P}{A}
    \label{eq:fair_spread}
\end{equation}

The result is converted to basis points by multiplying by 10{,}000:

\begin{equation}
    s^*_{\text{bps}} = \frac{P}{A} \times 10{,}000
    \label{eq:spread_bps}
\end{equation}

A floor is applied to ensure the spread does not fall below the minimum PRS spread:

\begin{equation}
    s_{\text{final}} = \max\!\big(s^*_{\text{bps}},\; s_{\text{min}}\big)
    \label{eq:spread_floor}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $s_{\text{min}} = 2$\,bp (the \texttt{MIN\_PRS\_SPREAD\_BPS} parameter,
calibrated to the FloodRE minimum insurance rate).

\subsubsection{Fallback Pricing}

If the risky annuity $A \leq 0$ (which can occur for extremely high hazard rates where
survival probability is negligible), the model falls back to a simple linear
approximation:

\begin{equation}
    s_{\text{fallback}} = \lambda_{\text{annual}} \times 10{,}000 \text{ bps}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Recovery Rate}

The recovery rate is hardcoded at $R = 0$ for all trigger levels, corresponding to
100\% loss given default ($\text{LGD} = 1 - R = 1$). Upon a trigger event the full
notional is paid out by the protection seller. No partial recovery is modelled.

\subsection{Data-Driven Basis}
\label{sec:basis}

Property-level PRS spreads are not computed by adding a parametric adjustment to gauge
spreads. Instead, each property has its own simulated flood history (from the upstream
property time-series module), its own GEV-fitted hazard rate, and its own fair spread
from the CDS pricing framework above. The basis is therefore an \emph{observed}
quantity:

\begin{equation}
    B = s_{\text{gauge}} - s_{\text{property}}
    \label{eq:basis_def}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $s_{\text{gauge}}$ and $s_{\text{property}}$ are the fair spreads at
a given tenor and trigger level. A positive basis indicates the property is safer than
the gauge (lower hazard rate); a negative basis indicates higher property risk.

The basis is driven by three physical mechanisms in the flood simulation, described
below.

\subsubsection{Distance Effect: Retention Model}

The gauge flood signal (water surface elevation, WSE) is attenuated as it propagates
laterally from the river to the property location. The retention model uses pure
exponential decay:

\begin{equation}
    \text{retention}(d) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{d}{L}\right)
    \label{eq:retention}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $d$ is the horizontal distance from the gauge to the property (metres)
and $L = 3{,}000$\,m is the characteristic retention length (e-folding distance). At
$d = L$ the retention factor is $1/e \approx 0.37$.

The attenuated WSE at the property is:

\begin{equation}
    \text{WSE}_{\text{prop}} = \text{WSE}_{\text{gauge}} \times \text{retention}(d)
    \label{eq:wse_prop}
\end{equation}

Greater distance reduces the number and severity of floods at the property, lowering
the property hazard rate and spread, and producing a positive basis.

Reference values:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item At 200\,m: retention $\approx$ 0.94
    \item At 1\,km: retention $\approx$ 0.72
    \item At 3\,km: retention $\approx$ 0.37
    \item At 5\,km: retention $\approx$ 0.19
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Elevation Effect}

Properties elevated above their reference gauge benefit from a higher flood threshold.
A flood event reaches the property only when the propagated WSE exceeds the property
ground elevation plus the floor level:

\begin{equation}
    d_{\text{flood}} = \max\!\big(0,\; \text{WSE}_{\text{prop}} - (h_{\text{prop}} - h_{\text{gauge}}) - h_{\text{floor}}\big)
    \label{eq:flood_depth}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $h_{\text{prop}}$ is the property ground elevation (mAOD),
$h_{\text{gauge}}$ is the gauge elevation, and $h_{\text{floor}}$ is the floor step
height. A higher elevation differential means fewer flood events exceed the threshold,
reducing the property hazard rate and producing a positive basis.

The elevation effect interacts with the distance effect: a property that is both far
from the gauge and at higher elevation benefits from both attenuation and a higher
threshold.

\subsubsection{Terrain: EA Flood Zone}

The Environment Agency flood zone classification (Zone~1, Zone~2, Zone~3, Zone~3a,
Zone~3b, Enhanced Defence) characterises the baseline flood risk at a location. In the
current model, terrain effects are captured \emph{implicitly} through the flood
simulation boundary conditions rather than as a parametric basis component. Properties
in higher-risk flood zones will tend to have higher simulated flood frequencies and
therefore higher spreads.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lp{9cm}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{EA Flood Zone} & \textbf{Risk Context} \\
        \midrule
        Zone 3b            & Functional floodplain --- highest risk \\
        Zone 3a / Zone 3   & High probability (1 in 100 year or greater) \\
        Zone 2             & Medium probability (1 in 100 to 1 in 1{,}000 year) \\
        Zone 1             & Low probability ($<$1 in 1{,}000 year) \\
        Enhanced Defence   & Behind formal flood defences --- reduced risk \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}

    \label{tab:terrain_context}

\subsection{Spread Decomposition via Synthetic Horizons}
\label{sec:decomposition}

To attribute the total basis to its physical components, the system generates two
synthetic simulation variants alongside the normal property run:

\begin{description}[nosep]
    \item[SHD (Synthetic Horizon --- Distance zero):] The property is simulated as if
        co-located with the gauge ($d = 0$, full retention), but at its true elevation.
        Output file: \texttt{propertyshd.json}.
    \item[SHE (Synthetic Horizon --- Elevation zero):] The property is simulated at its
        true distance but with its elevation set equal to the gauge elevation
        ($\Delta h = 0$). Output file: \texttt{propertyshe.json}.
\end{description}

Let $s_G$, $s_P$, $s_{\text{SHD}}$, $s_{\text{SHE}}$ denote the 5-year any-flood
spreads for the gauge, property, SHD synthetic, and SHE synthetic respectively.

\subsubsection{Distance-First Decomposition}

\begin{align}
    \text{Distance effect} &= s_{\text{SHE}} - s_G \label{eq:dist_first_d} \\
    \text{Elevation effect} &= s_P - s_{\text{SHE}} \label{eq:dist_first_e}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Elevation-First Decomposition}

\begin{align}
    \text{Elevation effect} &= s_{\text{SHD}} - s_G \label{eq:elev_first_e} \\
    \text{Distance effect} &= s_P - s_{\text{SHD}} \label{eq:elev_first_d}
\end{align}

Both paths satisfy the identity:

\begin{equation}
    \text{Distance effect} + \text{Elevation effect} = s_P - s_G = -B
    \label{eq:decomp_identity}
\end{equation}

\noindent The two paths yield different component magnitudes because distance and
elevation effects interact non-linearly in the flood simulation. The decomposition is
presented as two paths to provide bounds on each component's contribution.

\subsection{QuantLib Pricing (prs\_with\_hazard\_curve.py)}
\label{sec:quantlib}

An alternative pricing path is provided using the QuantLib library's CDS framework.
This serves as an independent cross-validation of the analytical model.

\subsubsection{Curve Construction}

The QuantLib path supports two modes:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Term structure mode}: A survival probability term structure is loaded
          from the hazard curve output. An implied constant hazard rate is extracted from
          the terminal survival probability:
          \begin{equation}
              \hat{\lambda} = \frac{-\ln S(T)}{T}
          \end{equation}
          and used to construct a \texttt{FlatHazardRate} curve (required for ISDA CDS
          engine compatibility).
    \item \textbf{Flat hazard mode}: A \texttt{FlatHazardRate} curve is constructed
          directly from the annual hazard rate $\lambda_{\text{annual}}$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{CDS Instrument Construction}

The QuantLib CDS is constructed with:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item TwentiethIMM date generation,
    \item TARGET calendar,
    \item Actual/360 day count for the premium leg,
    \item Actual/365~Fixed for the hazard and discount curves,
    \item Running spread set to 100\,bp (reference; fair spread derived from NPV).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Calibration}

The model parameters are calibrated as follows:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Hazard rate}: Derived from the GEV hazard curve model (see separate
          documentation). Not a free parameter of this model.
    \item \textbf{Recovery rate}: Hardcoded at zero (full loss given default). No recovery
          is assumed upon trigger event.
    \item \textbf{Risk-free rate}: Set to 3\% as a representative long-term UK gilt rate.
          Not dynamically calibrated to market curves.
    \item \textbf{Minimum spread}: Calibrated to the FloodRE Levy 2 minimum insurance
          premium rate.
    \item \textbf{Retention length}: Set to 3{,}000\,m based on Thames corridor hydraulic
          calibration. Controls the rate of flood signal attenuation with distance.
    \item \textbf{Basis}: Emergent from full flood simulation; not independently
          calibrated. Depends on the upstream hazard, propagation, and property
          simulation modules.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Two Pricing Paths}

The system provides two independent pricing implementations:

\begin{description}
    \item[Analytical path] (\texttt{compute\_prs\_spread}): A pure Python implementation
        that computes the risky annuity and protection leg via explicit summation over
        quarterly periods. This is the primary production path, used for all portfolio-level
        pricing and report generation. It has no dependency on QuantLib.
    \item[QuantLib path] (\texttt{price\_prs}): A QuantLib-based implementation that
        constructs a \texttt{CreditDefaultSwap} instrument and prices it using the
        \texttt{IsdaCdsEngine}. This path is used for model validation and produces
        additional outputs (NPV, premium leg NPV, protection leg NPV, fair upfront).
\end{description}

\subsection{Key Implementation Details}

\subsubsection{Hazard Rate Clamping}

The annual hazard rate is clamped to $[0, 0.999]$ before conversion to the continuous
rate. A rate of zero returns a spread of 0\,bp immediately (no floor applied, as a
zero hazard rate means no risk). The upper clamp prevents $\ln(0)$ in
Equation~\eqref{eq:hazard_conversion}.

\subsubsection{Synthetic Horizon Modes}

The flood propagation module (\texttt{propagation.py}) accepts a \texttt{mode} parameter:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \texttt{mode="normal"}: standard propagation using actual distance and elevation.
    \item \texttt{mode="shd"}: sets property elevation equal to gauge elevation
          ($\Delta h = 0$), isolating the distance effect.
    \item \texttt{mode="she"}: sets distance to zero (full retention), isolating the
          elevation effect.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{QuantLib Date Conventions}

The QuantLib implementation uses \texttt{ql.Date.todaysDate()} as the evaluation date,
TARGET calendar, Actual/360 day count for the premium leg, and Actual/365 Fixed for
the hazard and discount curves. The schedule is generated with TwentiethIMM date
generation, following standard CDS market conventions.

% 6. SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS
% ===================================================================
\mkmsensitivity

This section analyses the sensitivity of the PRS fair spread to each key input
parameter.

\subsection{Hazard Rate Sensitivity}

The fair spread is monotonically increasing in the annual hazard rate. For typical UK
flood probabilities ($\lambda_{\text{annual}} \in [0.001, 0.10]$), the relationship is
approximately linear for low hazard rates and becomes concave for higher rates due to
the survival probability declining faster.

\input{sensitivity_tables}

\subsection{Recovery Rate Sensitivity}

The fair spread is proportional to $(1-R)$ in the protection leg numerator. A higher
recovery rate reduces the spread linearly. For a fixed hazard rate of
$\lambda_{\text{annual}} = 0.01$:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial s^*}{\partial R} = -\frac{1}{A} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big[S(t_{i-1}) - S(t_i)\big] \cdot D\!\left(t_i - \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\right) < 0
\end{equation}

\subsection{Risk-Free Rate Sensitivity}

An increase in the risk-free rate $r$ reduces both the risky annuity and the protection
leg PV via higher discount factors. The net effect on the spread ratio $P/A$ is
typically small and slightly positive (spread increases with $r$), as the annuity
decreases faster than the protection leg.

\subsection{Tenor Sensitivity}

Longer tenors produce marginally higher spreads for a given hazard rate, as the
conditional default probability per period is constant but the accumulated survival
probability decreases. However, under a flat hazard curve, the fair spread converges
rapidly and is largely tenor-insensitive for tenors beyond 3--5 years.

\subsection{Distance Sensitivity}

The property spread decreases with distance from the gauge due to exponential
attenuation of the flood signal via the retention model
(Equation~\eqref{eq:retention}). Table~\ref{tab:sens_distance} shows the property
spread for a gauge at approximately 300\,bp, with the retention length $L = 3{,}000$\,m.

At 1\,km from the gauge, the property spread drops to approximately 214\,bp
(retention $\approx 0.72$). At 3\,km (one e-folding length) the spread falls to
approximately 109\,bp. Beyond 5\,km the property spread approaches the 2\,bp floor.

\subsection{Elevation Sensitivity}

The property spread decreases as the property elevation increases above the gauge,
because a higher flood threshold reduces the fraction of gauge floods that also flood
the property. Table~\ref{tab:sens_elevation} shows the property spread at distance
zero for increasing elevation differentials, using a characteristic depth scale of
2.0\,m (Thames corridor calibration).

At 2\,m above the gauge the spread falls to approximately 109\,bp; at 5\,m above it
drops to approximately 24\,bp. Beyond 10\,m of elevation the spread reaches the floor.

\subsection{Combined Distance and Elevation Sensitivity}

Table~\ref{tab:sens_combined} presents a matrix of property spreads across distance
and elevation combinations. The two effects are multiplicative: the property flood
probability scales as $\text{retention}(d) \times \exp(-\Delta h / h_{\text{scale}})$,
so the combined effect compounds. For example, a property 1\,km from the gauge and
2\,m above it has a spread of approximately 78\,bp, compared to 300\,bp at the gauge.

\subsection{Terrain Sensitivity (EA Flood Zone)}

The EA flood zone classification determines the baseline gauge hazard rate: properties
in higher-risk zones are served by gauges with higher annual flood probabilities.
Tables~\ref{tab:sens_terrain_a}--\ref{tab:sens_terrain_e} show the gauge spread and
resulting property spread for representative hazard rates per zone at a fixed distance
of 500\,m, across five elevation offsets from 0.5\,m to 2.5\,m above gauge.

At 0.5\,m above gauge (Table~\ref{tab:sens_terrain_a}), Zone~3b basis is 180\,bp;
at 2.5\,m (Table~\ref{tab:sens_terrain_e}) the same zone widens to 396\,bp as the
elevation decay reduces the property spread. Zone~1 basis ranges from 3.4\,bp at
0.5\,m to 7.6\,bp at 2.5\,m. The terrain effect is thus the dominant driver of
absolute spread levels, while distance and elevation determine the basis within a
given zone.

\subsubsection{EA Flood Zone Hazard Rates}

The following representative annual hazard rates are used per EA zone (midpoint of
published EA probability ranges):

\subsubsection{Interactive Terrain Repricing}

The terrain effect is implemented as a \textbf{relative spread delta}:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{\text{terrain}} = S(z_{\text{selected}}, d, h) - S(z_{\text{actual}}, d, h)
    \label{eq:terrain_delta}
\end{equation}
where $S(z, d, h)$ is the property spread at zone $z$, distance $d$, and elevation
$h$, computed by bilinear interpolation from a pre-computed grid. The grid is a dense
$(z \times d \times h)$ lookup with 21 distance points (0--5{,}000\,m in 250\,m steps)
and 11 elevation points (0--5\,m in 0.5\,m steps).

The baseline is the property's actual assigned zone. When the user selects a different
zone via the UI dropdown, $\Delta_{\text{terrain}}$ is applied additively to the
property spread. The waterfall decomposition becomes:
\begin{equation}
    s_{\text{property}}^{\text{adj}} = s_{\text{gauge}} + \Delta_{\text{terrain}}
    + \Delta_{\text{distance}} + \Delta_{\text{elevation}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Terrain Sensitivity by Distance}

Tables~\ref{tab:sens_terrain_dist_a}--\ref{tab:sens_terrain_dist_e} hold the elevation
fixed at 1.0\,m above gauge and vary the distance from 250\,m to 1{,}500\,m. For
Zone~3b, basis widens from 233\,bp at 250\,m to 331\,bp at 1{,}500\,m. The distance
effect is more pronounced in higher-risk zones where the gauge spread is larger: Zone~1
basis only moves from 4.4\,bp to 6.4\,bp over the same range.

\mkmvalidation

\subsection{Cross-Validation: Analytical vs QuantLib}

The primary validation approach is cross-validation between the two independent pricing
paths. For flat hazard rate inputs, the analytical and QuantLib implementations are
expected to agree to within 0.5\,bp for typical parameterisations.

\textbf{Methodology}: For a grid of hazard rates $\lambda_{\text{annual}} \in \{0.001,
0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.10\}$, tenors $T \in \{1, 3, 5, 10\}$ years, and recovery
$R = 0$, both pricing paths are evaluated and the
absolute spread difference is recorded.

\textbf{Expected result}: Differences arise from:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Day count convention differences (Actual/365 vs Actual/360 on the premium leg),
    \item Date generation differences (exact quarterly vs TwentiethIMM roll dates),
    \item Accrual and settlement date flow handling in the ISDA engine.
\end{itemize}

These differences are expected to be small ($<1$\,bp) and do not represent model risk.

\subsection{Spread Decomposition Validation}

The data-driven basis and SHD/SHE decomposition are validated through the following
checks:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Monotonicity}: Property spread is non-increasing with distance
          (holding elevation constant).
    \item \textbf{Monotonicity}: Property spread is non-increasing with elevation
          differential (holding distance constant).
    \item \textbf{Decomposition identity}: For each property,
          $\text{distance\_effect} + \text{elevation\_effect} = s_P - s_G$ for both
          the distance-first and elevation-first paths.
    \item \textbf{Sign consistency}: Distance effect and elevation effect are both
          non-positive (property is generally safer than gauge).
    \item \textbf{Range}: For typical Thames catchment properties, total basis falls
          within $[0, s_G]$\,bp (property cannot be riskier than the gauge under
          the single-gauge propagation model).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Comparison with FloodRE Indicative Rates}

As an external benchmark, the model's property-level PRS spreads are compared against
FloodRE Levy 2 indicative insurance premium rates for properties in equivalent flood
zones. The PRS spread is expected to be of similar order of magnitude to the FloodRE
rate, adjusted for differences in:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Contract structure (swap vs insurance),
    \item Loss given default assumptions,
    \item Expense loading (FloodRE includes administrative costs; PRS does not).
\end{itemize}

Exact numerical agreement is not expected. The comparison serves as a reasonableness
check only.


% ===================================================================
% 8. MODEL LIMITATIONS AND KNOWN WEAKNESSES
% ===================================================================
\mkmlimitations

\subsection{Constant Hazard Rate Assumption}

The analytical model assumes a constant (time-homogeneous) hazard rate over the full
contract tenor. This means:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{No climate trend}: The hazard rate does not increase over time to
          reflect projected increases in flood frequency due to climate change. For
          longer tenors ($>5$ years), this may understate the true fair spread.
    \item \textbf{No seasonality}: Flood risk is not adjusted for seasonal variation
          (winter vs summer). The annual hazard rate is an average over the full year.
    \item \textbf{No term structure}: The QuantLib path supports a piecewise survival
          curve, but the analytical path does not.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{No Counterparty Credit Risk}

The model prices the PRS on a risk-free basis with no credit valuation adjustment
(CVA) or debit valuation adjustment (DVA). In practice, the protection seller's
creditworthiness affects the true economic value of the contract.

\subsection{Quarterly Discretisation Error}

The continuous-time protection leg is approximated by quarterly discretisation, with
default assumed to occur at the midpoint of each quarter. For low hazard rates (typical
of UK flood risk), this approximation is accurate. For very high hazard rates
($\lambda_{\text{annual}} > 0.20$), the quarterly discretisation may introduce errors
of up to 2--3\,bp relative to continuous-time pricing.

\subsection{Flat Risk-Free Curve}

The risk-free discount curve is flat at the specified rate. In practice, the UK gilt
curve has a term structure that affects the present value of future cash flows. For a
5-year PRS, the difference between flat and market-calibrated discounting is typically
small ($<1$\,bp effect on fair spread).

\subsection{Decomposition Path Dependence}

The SHD/SHE spread decomposition yields different distance and elevation component
magnitudes depending on the order of attribution (distance-first vs elevation-first).
This arises because the two effects interact non-linearly in the flood simulation:
a property that is both far from the gauge and at high elevation has a compound
reduction in flood probability that cannot be uniquely attributed to either factor.
Both paths are presented to provide bounds on each component.

\subsection{No Correlation Modelling}

The model prices single-name PRS contracts independently. It does not model spatial
correlation of flood events across multiple properties or gauges, which would be
required for portfolio-level risk aggregation or tranche pricing.

\subsection{Recovery Rate Assumptions}

The recovery rate is fixed at $R = 0$ (zero recovery, full loss given default) for all
trigger levels and does not vary with property value, flood depth, or construction
standards. This is a conservative assumption that maximises the fair spread for a given
hazard rate.

% 9. GOVERNANCE VALIDATION QUESTIONS
% ===================================================================
\mkmgovernance

\mkmvqitem{1}{Ronseal Test}{Does the model do what it says on the tin?}
Yes. The model prices Physical Risk Swap contracts by computing fair annual spreads in basis points using a CDS-equivalent quarterly premium framework. It delivers: (i)~gauge-level PRS pricing from hazard curve term structures, (ii)~property-level pricing from simulated property flood hazard rates, (iii)~basis decomposition into distance and elevation components via SHD/SHE synthetic horizons. Both the analytical and QuantLib pricing paths produce consistent results.

\mkmvqitem{2}{Accuracy and Stability}{Does the model provide timely, accurate and stable results?}
The analytical pricing engine produces identical results to the QuantLib ISDA CDS engine to within rounding tolerance for flat hazard rate inputs. The data-driven basis is derived directly from simulated property hazard rates, avoiding parametric approximation errors. Spreads are monotonically increasing in hazard rate, ensuring economic consistency. Sensitivity analysis confirms stable behaviour across the full range of input parameters. Pricing completes in under 1\,ms per contract (analytical path) or under 10\,ms (QuantLib path).

\mkmvqitem{3}{Resource Usage}{Does the model consume excessive computational resources?}
No. The analytical pricing path is $O(1)$ per contract with no external dependencies. The QuantLib path requires the QuantLib library but remains lightweight ($O(N_{\text{periods}})$ per contract where $N_{\text{periods}}$ is the number of quarterly payment dates). Full portfolio pricing (1,000 contracts) completes in under 1 second on either path. Memory usage is negligible. The SHD/SHE synthetic horizons require two additional simulation runs per portfolio, adding proportional but bounded overhead.

\mkmvqitem{4}{Override Controls}{Are there controls over manual overrides?}
The model does not support manual override of computed fair spreads. Contract parameters (tenor, recovery rate, risk-free rate, trigger level) are specified at invocation and are not modifiable post-computation. The MRC can apply a risk rating override via the governance dashboard, but this affects the model's governance status, not its computational outputs. Propagation parameters (retention length, Manning's roughness) are defined as fixed constants; changes require code modification.

\mkmvqitem{5}{Output Consumption}{How is the output consumed for risk decision-making?}
PRS spreads are the primary output used for: (a)~swap pricing and quotation in the Physical Risk market; (b)~mortgage credit risk adjustment via flood-adjusted hazard rates; (c)~basis risk analysis for hedging effectiveness assessment; (d)~stress testing via scenario-dependent hazard curves. Results are displayed in the PRS pricing panel of the interactive governance dashboard and exported in PDF property reports.

\mkmvqitem{6}{Model Chaining}{Is the model's output used as input to another model?}
Yes. Downstream, PRS spreads feed the Mortgage Credit Risk Pricer (MKM-MORT-001) for flood-adjusted expected loss calculation. Basis analysis outputs inform hedging strategy decisions. Upstream, the model consumes term structures from the GEV Hazard Curve Model (MKM-HAZARD-001) and property-level hazard rates from the Property Hazard Curve module. The flood propagation model provides the gauge-to-property attenuation via the retention model.

\mkmvqitem{7}{Weaknesses}{Does the model have weaknesses and limitations of use?}
Yes; the principal weaknesses are documented in Section~8. Key limitations: (i)~flat hazard rate assumption over the contract tenor (no time-varying climate risk); (ii)~SHD/SHE spread decomposition is path-dependent (distance-first vs elevation-first yield different component magnitudes); (iii)~quarterly premium schedule is CDS-standard and may not match bespoke contract terms; (iv)~the recovery rate is treated as a fixed constant rather than a stochastic variable. The model prices single-name contracts only; portfolio-level PRS structuring is out of scope.

\mkmvqitem{8}{Monitoring}{What type of monitoring is required?}
The following monitoring is recommended: (a)~quarterly comparison of model spreads against any available market transaction data; (b)~annual review of retention model calibration ($L = 3{,}000$\,m) against property flood loss experience; (c)~cross-validation between analytical and QuantLib pricing paths following library updates; (d)~sensitivity analysis of spread to recovery rate and risk-free rate assumptions; (e)~stress testing of spreads under all five intensity scenario families; (f)~verification that SHD/SHE decomposition identities hold across the portfolio.

\mkmvqitem{9}{Overall Risk}{Is the overall model risk acceptable?}
Conditionally acceptable. As a Tier~1 model with direct pricing impact, the PRS Pricing Engine carries significant model risk. The flat hazard rate assumption is the primary concern: under climate change scenarios, hazard rates are likely to increase over multi-year tenors, leading to underpriced protection. This is partially mitigated by conservative scenario selection and the retention model safety margin. The path-dependent decomposition is a known limitation accepted by the MRC pending future enhancement.
