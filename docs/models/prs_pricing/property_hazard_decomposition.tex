% 1. OVERVIEW
% ===================================================================
\mkmexecsummary

This document describes the construction of property-level hazard curves and the
decomposition of the gauge-to-property basis into distance and elevation components.
The approach uses four parallel simulation outputs (propertyts, propertytsd,
propertytse, propertyhc) to isolate each physical factor, enabling a transparent
waterfall from gauge spread to property spread.

Two randomly selected properties from the generated portfolio are used as worked
examples, with full numerical detail at each stage of the pipeline.

% ===================================================================
% 2. PIPELINE ARCHITECTURE
% ===================================================================
\section{Pipeline Architecture}

\subsection{Generation Sequence}

The portfolio generation pipeline produces gauges, synthetic gauges, properties, and
their flood simulations in the following order:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Real gauges} (52 gauges on the Thames) --- \texttt{gauge.json}
    \item \textbf{Synthetic gauges} (200 virtual gauges placed at random positions
          along the river centreline, snapped to the high-resolution river polyline)
          --- appended to \texttt{gauge.json}
    \item \textbf{Properties} (200, each placed perpendicular to its assigned
          synthetic gauge at 100--2{,}000\,m distance) --- \texttt{property.json}
    \item \textbf{Gauge historical data} (50 years per gauge) --- \texttt{gaugehd/}
    \item \textbf{Storm sequences} (20{,}000 multi-storm sequences, spatial
          propagation across all 252 gauges) --- \texttt{gaugets/}
    \item \textbf{Gauge hazard curves} (GEV fit per gauge) --- \texttt{gaugehc.json}
    \item \textbf{Property flood time series} --- four variants (Section~\ref{sec:four_modes})
    \item \textbf{Property hazard curves + PRS pricing} --- four variants
    \item \textbf{Spread decomposition} (post-processing attachment)
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Synthetic Gauge as Controlling Boundary}

Each property is anchored to a single \emph{synthetic gauge} on the river centreline.
The synthetic gauge:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Is placed at a random position on the gauge-point polyline, weighted by
          segment length for uniform spatial coverage;
    \item Is snapped to the high-resolution river polyline (2{,}645 points) to ensure
          it sits on the actual river channel;
    \item Has its elevation, flood alert, warning, and severe levels interpolated
          linearly from the two flanking real gauges;
    \item Receives storm responses through the same spatial propagation model as real
          gauges (it is a first-class entity in \texttt{gauge.json}).
\end{itemize}

The property is then placed at a perpendicular distance of 100--2{,}000\,m from the
synthetic gauge, on a random side of the river. Its elevation is:

\begin{equation}
    h_{\text{prop}} = h_{\text{synth}} + \Delta h
    \label{eq:prop_elev}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\Delta h = (d / 1{,}000) \times g$, with $d$ the perpendicular
distance in metres and $g \sim U(2, 5)$\,m/km a random floodplain gradient.

The EA flood zone is derived from $\Delta h$ using the boundary table in
Table~\ref{tab:zone_bounds}.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{EA flood zone classification from vertical offset above synthetic gauge.}
    \label{tab:zone_bounds}
    \begin{tabular}{llr}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Zone} & \textbf{Offset range} & \textbf{EA annual probability} \\
        \midrule
        Zone 3b & $[0.0, 0.5)$\,m & Functional floodplain \\
        Zone 3a & $[0.5, 1.5)$\,m & $> 1$ in 100 \\
        Zone 2  & $[1.5, 3.0)$\,m & 1 in 100 to 1 in 1{,}000 \\
        Zone 1  & $\geq 3.0$\,m   & $< 1$ in 1{,}000 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

% 3. FOUR SIMULATION MODES
% ===================================================================
\section{Four Simulation Modes}
\label{sec:four_modes}

For each property, the flood time series and hazard curve are computed in four modes.
Each mode zeroes out one physical factor to isolate the other, enabling a clean
decomposition of the gauge-to-property basis.

\subsection{Mode Definitions}

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Simulation modes and their overrides.}
    \label{tab:modes}
    \begin{tabular}{llllp{5cm}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Mode} & \textbf{Code} & \textbf{Distance} & \textbf{Elevation} & \textbf{Purpose} \\
        \midrule
        Normal     & \texttt{propertyts / propertyhc}   & Real $d$  & Real $h_{\text{prop}}$ & Full property hazard \\
        SHD        & \texttt{propertytsd / propertyshd}  & Real $d$  & $h_{\text{synth}}$ (zero diff) & Isolate distance effect \\
        SHE        & \texttt{propertytse / propertyshe}  & $d = 0$   & Real $h_{\text{prop}}$ & Isolate elevation effect \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{SHD --- Synthetic Height-Distance}

SHD sets the property elevation equal to the synthetic gauge elevation, removing the
elevation barrier. The property still sits at its real distance from the gauge, so the
only attenuation is the exponential distance decay:

\begin{equation}
    \text{retention}(d) = e^{-d / L}, \quad L = 3{,}000\,\text{m}
\end{equation}

\noindent Because the elevation threshold is zero, every storm that produces any water
at the property location (after distance decay) will flood it. This gives the
\emph{maximum flood count} achievable at the property's distance.

Since the property is at gauge level, SHD is assigned \textbf{Zone~3b} (functional
floodplain, offset = 0\,m).

\subsection{SHE --- Synthetic Height-Elevation}

SHE sets the distance to zero (property collocated with the gauge), removing the
distance decay. The property retains its real elevation, so the only barrier is the
height difference:

\begin{equation}
    \text{flood threshold} = \max(0,\; h_{\text{prop}} - h_{\text{synth}}) + h_{\text{floor}}
\end{equation}

\noindent The gauge's full water surface elevation (WSE) is applied directly. Only storms
producing WSE above the threshold flood the property. This gives the flood count
achievable with \emph{no distance attenuation}.

SHE retains the property's real EA flood zone (derived from the actual elevation offset).


% ===================================================================
% 4. HAZARD CURVE CONSTRUCTION
% ===================================================================
\section{Hazard Curve Construction}

\subsection{Flood Event Extraction}

For each mode, the simulation produces a set of flood events from the 20{,}000 storm
sequences. Each event records:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Whether the property flooded (binary)
    \item Flood depth (m) if flooded
    \item Attenuated water surface elevation (m AOD)
    \item Damage ratio (from the depth-damage curve)
\end{itemize}

\noindent The \emph{flood count} is the number of storms that produced a non-zero flood
depth at the property.

\subsection{GEV Distribution Fitting}

If the property has $\geq 3$ flood events, a Generalised Extreme Value (GEV)
distribution is fitted to the flood depths using maximum likelihood:

\begin{equation}
    F(x; \mu, \sigma, \xi) =
    \exp\!\left\{-\left[1 + \xi\left(\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right)\right]^{-1/\xi}\right\}
\end{equation}

\noindent The annual exceedance probability at each depth threshold $\tau$ is:

\begin{equation}
    P_{\text{annual}}(\tau) = \frac{n_{\text{floods}}}{N_{\text{storms}}} \times
    P(X > \tau \mid X > 0)
    \label{eq:annual_prob}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $n_{\text{floods}} / N_{\text{storms}}$ is the base rate (fraction of
storms causing any flooding) and $P(X > \tau \mid X > 0)$ is the conditional exceedance
from the GEV fit.

If fewer than 3 flood events, the property falls back to the minimum spread floor
(2.0\,bp).

\subsection{PRS Spread Calculation}

The annual hazard rate feeds a quarterly CDS-equivalent pricer:

\begin{equation}
    s = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{4T} (1-R)\,(S_{i-1} - S_i)\,\text{DF}_i}
             {\sum_{i=1}^{4T} \Delta t \cdot S_i \cdot \text{DF}_i}
    \times 10{,}000 \;\text{(bp)}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $S_i = e^{-\lambda \cdot t_i}$ is the survival probability, $\text{DF}_i = e^{-r \cdot t_i}$
is the discount factor, $R = 0$ is the recovery rate, $T = 5$ years is the tenor, and
$\Delta t = 0.25$ years.


% ===================================================================
% 5. SPREAD DECOMPOSITION
% ===================================================================
\section{Spread Decomposition}
\label{sec:decomp}

The basis between the gauge and the property is decomposed into distance and elevation
effects using the SHD and SHE synthetic horizons. Let:

\begin{align}
    s_G &= \text{Gauge 5-year severe spread (bp)} \\
    s_P &= \text{Property 5-year severe spread (bp)} \\
    s_{\text{SHD}} &= \text{SHD spread (real distance, zero elevation diff)} \\
    s_{\text{SHE}} &= \text{SHE spread (zero distance, real elevation)}
\end{align}

\noindent The total basis is:

\begin{equation}
    B = s_G - s_P
\end{equation}

\noindent Because distance and elevation interact non-linearly in the flood simulation,
the decomposition depends on the order of attribution. Two paths are computed:

\subsection{Path 1: Distance First}

\begin{align}
    \text{Distance effect} &= s_{\text{SHE}} - s_G \\
    \text{Elevation effect} &= s_P - s_{\text{SHE}} \\
    \text{Check:}\quad s_G + \text{DE} + \text{EE} &= s_P \quad \checkmark
\end{align}

\noindent Interpretation: start at the gauge, apply distance decay (SHE still has the
full elevation barrier but zero distance), then apply the elevation barrier to reach the
property.

\subsection{Path 2: Elevation First}

\begin{align}
    \text{Elevation effect} &= s_{\text{SHD}} - s_G \\
    \text{Distance effect} &= s_P - s_{\text{SHD}} \\
    \text{Check:}\quad s_G + \text{EE} + \text{DE} &= s_P \quad \checkmark
\end{align}

\noindent Interpretation: start at the gauge, remove the elevation barrier (SHD sits at
gauge level), then apply distance decay to reach the property.

\subsection{Waterfall Diagram}

The spread decomposition is presented as a waterfall:

\begin{verbatim}
    Gauge Spread           s_G        (baseline)
      + Distance Effect    DE         (SHE - Gauge)
      + Elevation Effect   EE         (Property - SHE)
    = Property Spread      s_P
\end{verbatim}

\noindent Both effects are typically negative (reducing spread from gauge to property),
since distance and elevation both attenuate the flood signal.

% 7. INTERPRETATION GUIDE
% ===================================================================
\section{Interpretation Guide}

\subsection{Reading the Waterfall}

The waterfall traces how the gauge spread (the flood risk at the river) translates to
the property spread (the flood risk at the building). Both decomposition paths must sum
to the same property spread --- this is verified automatically by the pipeline.

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Gauge spread} is the baseline: the PRS price at the synthetic gauge on
          the river centreline.
    \item \textbf{Distance effect} is always negative: the exponential retention decay
          reduces the flood signal as it propagates from the river to the property.
    \item \textbf{Elevation effect} is always negative: the property sits above the gauge,
          raising the flood threshold and reducing the number of events that breach it.
    \item \textbf{Property spread} is the residual after both effects are applied.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why Two Paths?}

Distance and elevation are not independent --- they interact in the flood physics. A
storm that is attenuated by distance may or may not clear the elevation threshold,
depending on its original intensity. The two paths provide \emph{bounds} on each
component's contribution:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Path 1 (distance first) tends to attribute more to distance, because the
          elevation effect is computed after the signal has already been attenuated.
    \item Path 2 (elevation first) tends to attribute more to elevation, because the
          distance effect is computed after the elevation barrier has already filtered
          out weaker storms.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The ``true'' contribution of each factor lies between the two path estimates.

\subsection{Expected Spread Ranges}

Based on the sensitivity analysis (PRS Pricing Model Spec v2.0, Tables 4--6):

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item A gauge at 300\,bp with a property at 500\,m and 1\,m above: property spread
          $\approx$ 100--120\,bp (basis $\approx$ 180--200\,bp).
    \item A gauge at 300\,bp with a property at 1{,}000\,m and 2\,m above: property
          spread $\approx$ 40--60\,bp (basis $\approx$ 240--260\,bp).
    \item Zone 3b property (0--0.5\,m above, $<$500\,m): should retain most of the gauge
          spread, with basis $<$ 100\,bp.
\end{itemize}
