% 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
% ===================================================================
\mkmexecsummary

This document presents the mathematical framework, implementation, and validation of the
Gauge Historical Daily (GaugeHD) Synthetic Generation Model (MKM-GHD-001). The model
generates realistic daily water level timeseries for flood gauges in the Thames catchment,
producing 50-year synthetic histories that serve as the baseline for downstream hazard
analysis, stress testing, and PRS pricing.

The central design objective is to produce daily water levels that are statistically
consistent with the gauge's flood thresholds (FloodAlert, FloodWarning, SevereFloodWarning),
historical high records, and geographic tidal characteristics. The model classifies each
gauge into one of three tidal regimes---\emph{Non-tidal}, \emph{Partially tidal}, or
\emph{Tidal}---based on the gauge's longitude relative to Teddington Lock, and applies
regime-specific parameters controlling seasonal amplitude, tidal oscillation, daily noise,
and autocorrelation.

Five stochastic components are combined additively: (i)~a base level calibrated to the
gauge's FloodAlert threshold, (ii)~a seasonal cosine with winter peak, (iii)~a spring-neap
tidal cycle for tidally-influenced gauges, (iv)~an AR(1) autocorrelated noise process, and
(v)~Poisson-distributed flood event injection. Historical high levels are placed at their
recorded dates when available.

\textbf{Key risk:} The synthetic timeseries feeds the monthly means computation used by the
stress model pipeline (stressm) and provides the historical baseline for GEV fitting. An
incorrect tidal classification or mis-calibrated autocorrelation coefficient will distort
the variance structure of the generated series, leading to biased flood frequency estimates
downstream.

% 2. MODEL PURPOSE AND SCOPE
% ===================================================================
\mkmpurpose

\subsection{Purpose}

The GaugeHD Synthetic Generation Model serves three principal purposes within the
Physical Risk platform:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Historical baseline generation.} Provide a 50-year daily water level
          timeseries for each gauge, establishing the distributional baseline from which
          flood exceedance statistics are derived.
    \item \textbf{Monthly means for stress testing.} The generated timeseries is
          summarised into monthly mean water levels, which are consumed by the stress
          model pipeline (\texttt{stressm}) as a baseline for scenario construction.
    \item \textbf{Level statistics.} Compute percentiles, annual maxima, flood
          exceedance counts, and seasonal patterns that inform gauge-level hazard
          curve calibration and validation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{In scope:} Univariate daily water level generation; tidal
          classification; seasonal, tidal, and AR(1) noise components; Poisson flood
          injection; historical high date placement; level statistics computation.
    \item \textbf{Out of scope:} Sub-daily (hourly/15-minute) timeseries; spatial
          correlation across gauges; rainfall--runoff modelling; real-time data
          assimilation; tidal harmonic analysis beyond the spring-neap envelope.
    \item \textbf{Upstream dependencies:} Gauge portfolio (\texttt{gauge.json}) providing
          flood thresholds, historical high levels, and geographic coordinates; CDM schema
          defining the \texttt{TidalInfluence} field.
    \item \textbf{Downstream consumers:} Stress model pipeline (\texttt{stressm});
          GEV hazard curve builder (MKM-HAZARD-001); gauge timeseries visualisation;
          Trading Desk historical tab.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Regulatory Context}

This model is classified as a \emph{Tier~2} component under the internal model
governance framework. It does not directly produce a regulated output but is a
material input to the hazard curve and stress testing models, both of which are
Tier~1. Accordingly, it is subject to annual independent validation and parameter
review.

% ===================================================================
% 3. MATHEMATICAL FRAMEWORK
% ===================================================================
\mkmframework

\subsection{Overview}

The synthetic water level at day $t$ is constructed as the sum of five components:
\begin{equation}
    W_t = \mu_0 + S_t + T_t + \varepsilon_t + F_t
    \label{eq:total_level}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mu_0$ is the base level, $S_t$ is the seasonal component, $T_t$ is the
tidal component, $\varepsilon_t$ is the AR(1) autocorrelated noise, and $F_t$ is the
flood injection term. A floor of $W_{\min} = 0.1$\,m is applied to prevent physically
meaningless negative levels.

\subsection{Tidal Classification}
\label{sec:tidal_class}

Each gauge is classified into one of three tidal regimes based on its longitude
$\lambda$ relative to Teddington Lock, the tidal limit of the Thames:

\begin{equation}
    \text{TidalInfluence} =
    \begin{cases}
        \text{Tidal}            & \text{if } \lambda > -0.25 \\
        \text{Partially tidal}  & \text{if } -0.35 < \lambda \le -0.25 \\
        \text{Non-tidal}        & \text{if } \lambda \le -0.35
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:tidal_class}
\end{equation}

\noindent The longitude thresholds are calibrated to the Thames geography. Teddington Lock
sits at approximately $\lambda \approx -0.32$; the classification bands provide a
transition zone to account for partially dampened tidal influence in the reach immediately
upstream.

The tidal classification is stored in the CDM field
\texttt{SensorDetails.GaugeInformation.TidalInfluence} and determines the parameter set
used for all subsequent model components.

\subsection{Base Level}

The base level $\mu_0$ is drawn uniformly from a fraction of the FloodAlert threshold
$h_{\text{alert}}$:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_0 = h_{\text{alert}} \cdot U(0.3,\, 0.5)
    \label{eq:base_level}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $U(a, b)$ denotes a uniform random variable on $[a, b]$. This ensures the
typical water level sits well below the alert threshold, consistent with observed gauge
behaviour.

\subsection{Seasonal Component}

The seasonal variation captures the winter-dominant rainfall pattern in the Thames
catchment. The seasonal component at day $t$ is:
\begin{equation}
    S_t = A_s \cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi(d_t - 15)}{365}\right)
    \label{eq:seasonal}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $d_t$ is the day-of-year (1--365) at time $t$, and $A_s$ is the
seasonal amplitude. The phase offset of 15 days positions the peak in mid-January,
reflecting the typical Thames winter high-water season. The seasonal amplitude varies
by tidal type (Table~\ref{tab:tidal_params}).

\subsection{Tidal Component}

For tidally-influenced gauges, a spring-neap modulation is applied with a period of
approximately 14.8 days (the synodic half-month):
\begin{equation}
    T_t = A_{\tau} \cdot M_t \cdot U_t(-1, 1)
    \label{eq:tidal}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $A_{\tau}$ is the tidal amplitude, $U_t(-1, 1)$ is an independent
uniform draw representing the semi-diurnal tidal phase (aggregated to daily resolution),
and $M_t$ is the spring-neap modulation envelope:
\begin{equation}
    M_t = 0.6 + 0.4\,\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi\,(d_t \bmod 14.8)}{14.8}\right)
    \label{eq:spring_neap}
\end{equation}

\noindent The modulation oscillates between 0.2 (neap tides, minimum amplitude) and 1.0
(spring tides, maximum amplitude). For non-tidal gauges, $A_{\tau} = 0$ and $T_t = 0$
identically.

\subsection{AR(1) Autocorrelated Noise}
\label{sec:ar1}

Daily water level fluctuations exhibit persistence: today's deviation from the mean is
correlated with yesterday's. This is modelled as a first-order autoregressive process:
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_t = \phi\,\varepsilon_{t-1} + \eta_t
    \label{eq:ar1}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\phi \in (0, 1)$ is the autocorrelation coefficient and $\eta_t$ is a
Gaussian innovation:
\begin{equation}
    \eta_t \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\; \sigma^2_w (1 - \phi^2)\right)
    \label{eq:innovation}
\end{equation}

\noindent The innovation variance is scaled by $(1 - \phi^2)$ so that the unconditional
(marginal) standard deviation of $\varepsilon_t$ equals $\sigma_w$, the daily noise
standard deviation. This ensures that the total variability of the generated series is
controlled by $\sigma_w$ regardless of the autocorrelation strength.

The autocorrelation coefficient $\phi$ varies by tidal type:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Non-tidal} ($\phi = 0.85$): High persistence. Upstream river levels
          change slowly, driven by sustained rainfall and baseflow.
    \item \textbf{Partially tidal} ($\phi = 0.75$): Moderate persistence. Tidal signal
          introduces some daily reset but rainfall effects still dominate.
    \item \textbf{Tidal} ($\phi = 0.6$): Lower persistence. The semi-diurnal tidal cycle
          resets the level twice daily, reducing day-to-day correlation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Flood Event Injection}

Flood events are injected as Poisson arrivals at two severity levels:

\subsubsection{Severe Floods}

Severe flood events occur with daily probability:
\begin{equation}
    p_{\text{severe}} = \frac{\max(n_3,\, 1)}{N}
    \label{eq:severe_prob}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $n_3$ is the historical count of Level~3 exceedances
(\texttt{FrequencyExceedLevel3} from the gauge portfolio) and $N = 365 \times Y$ is the
total number of days in the simulation ($Y = 50$ years by default). On a severe flood
day, the water level is drawn uniformly:
\begin{equation}
    W_t \sim U\!\left(h_{\text{severe}},\; 0.95\,h_{\text{max}}\right)
    \label{eq:severe_level}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $h_{\text{severe}}$ is the SevereFloodWarning threshold and $h_{\text{max}}$
is the historical high level.

\subsubsection{Minor Floods}

Minor flood events occur at five times the severe rate:
\begin{equation}
    p_{\text{minor}} = 5\,p_{\text{severe}}
    \label{eq:minor_prob}
\end{equation}

\noindent and produce water levels in the alert-to-warning range:
\begin{equation}
    W_t \sim U\!\left(h_{\text{alert}},\; h_{\text{warning}}\right)
    \label{eq:minor_level}
\end{equation}

Both flood types reset the AR(1) state: $\varepsilon_t \leftarrow W_t - \mu_0$, allowing
the autocorrelation process to decay naturally from the elevated flood level.

\subsection{Historical High Date Injection}

If the gauge record includes a \texttt{HistoricalHighDate} that falls within the
simulation window, the model places the exact historical high level at that date:
\begin{equation}
    W_{t^*} = h_{\text{max}} \quad \text{where } t^* = \texttt{HistoricalHighDate}
    \label{eq:hist_high}
\end{equation}

\noindent The AR(1) state is reset to $\varepsilon_{t^*} = h_{\text{max}} - \mu_0$ so
that subsequent days decay naturally from the peak. If the historical high date falls
outside the simulation window, it is not injected.

\subsection{Output Floor}

All generated levels are floored at $W_{\min} = 0.1$\,m:
\begin{equation}
    W_t \leftarrow \max(0.1,\; W_t)
    \label{eq:floor}
\end{equation}

\noindent This prevents negative or near-zero levels that would be physically unrealistic
for a permanently gauged river reach.

\subsection{Calibration Approach}

The tidal-regime parameters (Table~\ref{tab:tidal_params}) are calibrated by expert
judgement informed by the following physical reasoning:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Non-tidal gauges} are upstream and dominated by rainfall--runoff
          dynamics. Daily variability is low ($\sigma_w / \mu_0 = 0.08$) because
          baseflow provides a stable level. Seasonal variation is large ($A_s / \mu_0 = 0.30$)
          because winter rainfall is the primary driver. Autocorrelation is high
          ($\phi = 0.85$) because river levels respond slowly to catchment inputs.
    \item \textbf{Tidal gauges} are downstream and dominated by the semi-diurnal tide.
          Daily variability is high ($\sigma_w / \mu_0 = 0.20$) due to the tidal range.
          Seasonal variation is smaller ($A_s / \mu_0 = 0.20$) relative to the tidal
          signal. Autocorrelation is moderate ($\phi = 0.6$) because the tide resets
          levels twice daily.
    \item \textbf{Partially tidal gauges} interpolate between these extremes.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The flood injection probabilities are calibrated per-gauge from the historical
Level~3 exceedance count in the gauge portfolio, ensuring that each gauge's synthetic
history reproduces the observed frequency of severe events.

\mkmimplementation

\subsection{Software Architecture}

The model is implemented across two layers:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \texttt{src/models/statistics/synthetic.py} --- the core
          \texttt{generate\_synthetic\_timeseries()} function containing all mathematical
          logic. Depends only on the Python standard library (\texttt{math},
          \texttt{random}, \texttt{datetime}).
    \item \texttt{src/port/src/gauge/gaugehd/} --- the generator package providing the
          \texttt{GaugeHistoricalDaily} facade class, portfolio loading, statistics
          computation, NRFA data parsing, and batch runner.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Module Structure}

The \texttt{gaugehd} package comprises six modules:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \texttt{\_\_init\_\_.py} --- package facade, exports \texttt{GaugeHistoricalDaily}
          class.
    \item \texttt{generator.py} --- \texttt{GaugeHistoricalDaily} class delegating to
          sub-modules.
    \item \texttt{synthetic.py} --- \texttt{generate\_from\_gauge\_portfolio()}: orchestrates
          timeseries generation, statistics computation, and JSON output.
    \item \texttt{statistics.py} --- re-exports from \texttt{models.statistics.timeseries}.
    \item \texttt{nrfa.py} --- NRFA GDF CSV parser for real gauge data ingestion.
    \item \texttt{runner.py} --- batch generation (\texttt{generate\_all\_gauge\_histories()})
          and CLI entry point.
    \item \texttt{loader.py} --- gauge portfolio loading from \texttt{gauge.json}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dependency-Free Design}

The core generation function uses only the Python standard library:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \texttt{math.cos}, \texttt{math.pi} for seasonal and tidal oscillations.
    \item \texttt{random.gauss} for AR(1) innovations.
    \item \texttt{random.uniform} for base level, tidal phase, and flood level draws.
    \item \texttt{random.random} for Poisson flood event arrivals.
    \item \texttt{datetime} for date arithmetic and formatting.
\end{itemize}

\noindent This eliminates deployment dependencies on NumPy or SciPy, consistent with the
platform's design philosophy.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

Each gauge's timeseries is generated with a deterministic seed derived from its
\texttt{GaugeID}:
\begin{equation}
    \text{seed} = \texttt{hash(gauge\_id)} \bmod 2^{32}
    \label{eq:seed}
\end{equation}

\noindent This ensures that regeneration produces identical output for the same gauge
portfolio, supporting audit reproducibility.

\subsection{Output Format}

Each gauge produces a JSON file \texttt{gauge\_\{gauge\_id\}\_hd.json} in the
\texttt{gaugehd/} directory with the following structure:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \texttt{schema\_version}: ``1.0''
    \item \texttt{data\_type}: ``GaugeHistoricalDaily''
    \item \texttt{gauge\_metadata}: gauge identity, coordinates, flood stages
    \item \texttt{statistics}: computed level statistics (mean, max, percentiles,
          monthly means, annual maxima, flood exceedance counts)
    \item \texttt{daily\_observations}: array of \texttt{\{date, level\_meters\}} records
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Monthly Means Output}

The statistics module computes monthly mean water levels from the generated daily
observations. These monthly means are consumed by the stress model pipeline
(\texttt{stressm/gauge\_parser.py}) as the baseline water level for each month,
against which stress scenarios are constructed. The monthly means inherit the
seasonal pattern from equation~\eqref{eq:seasonal}, with January means approximately
$A_s$ above the annual mean and July means approximately $A_s$ below.

% ===================================================================
% 6. SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS
% ===================================================================
\mkmsensitivity

The model has four primary sensitivity dimensions: the AR(1) coefficient $\phi$,
the seasonal amplitude $A_s$, the tidal amplitude $A_{\tau}$, and the daily noise
standard deviation $\sigma_w$.

\subsection{Sensitivity to AR(1) Coefficient ($\phi$)}

The autocorrelation coefficient is the most influential parameter for the variance
structure of the generated series. The lag-$k$ autocorrelation of the AR(1) component
is $\rho_k = \phi^k$, so higher $\phi$ produces longer-memory behaviour:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item At $\phi = 0.85$ (non-tidal), the autocorrelation at lag 7 (one week) is
          $0.85^7 \approx 0.32$, meaning week-to-week persistence is significant.
    \item At $\phi = 0.6$ (tidal), the lag-7 autocorrelation is $0.6^7 \approx 0.028$,
          meaning weekly persistence is negligible.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The innovation variance scaling $(1 - \phi^2)$ ensures that the marginal
variance is constant across tidal types for the same $\sigma_w$. However, the
\emph{effective} number of independent observations in the series decreases with
higher $\phi$, amplifying sampling uncertainty in tail statistics.

\subsection{Sensitivity to Seasonal Amplitude ($A_s$)}

The seasonal amplitude directly controls the range of monthly mean levels. For non-tidal
gauges ($A_s / \mu_0 = 0.30$), the winter-to-summer swing is approximately $0.6\,\mu_0$.
For tidal gauges ($A_s / \mu_0 = 0.20$), the swing is $0.4\,\mu_0$. Since monthly means
feed the stress pipeline, an over-estimated $A_s$ will produce artificially low summer
baselines that may distort stress scenario magnitudes.

\subsection{Sensitivity to Tidal Amplitude ($A_{\tau}$)}

The tidal amplitude affects only tidally-influenced gauges. At $A_{\tau} / \mu_0 = 0.15$,
the spring-tide daily range is approximately $0.3\,\mu_0$ (peak-to-trough), contributing
substantially to the total variance. Since the tidal component uses a uniform random phase
(not a deterministic tidal harmonic), it introduces additional variance but no systematic
bias.

\subsection{Sensitivity to Flood Injection Rate}

The severe flood probability $p_{\text{severe}}$ is calibrated from the gauge's historical
Level~3 exceedance count. Gauges with $n_3 = 0$ default to $n_3 = 1$ (minimum one severe
event in 50 years). The minor flood rate is always five times the severe rate. These
rates are the primary control on the upper tail of the generated water level distribution.

\subsection{Recommended Sensitivity Tests}

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item Vary $\phi$ from 0.4 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05 for a representative non-tidal
          gauge. Report the lag-1 to lag-30 autocorrelation function and the empirical
          flood exceedance frequency.
    \item Vary $A_s / \mu_0$ from 0.10 to 0.40 in steps of 0.05. Report monthly mean
          levels and the January/July ratio.
    \item For a tidal gauge, vary $A_{\tau} / \mu_0$ from 0.05 to 0.25. Report total
          daily variance and the fraction of variance attributable to the tidal component.
    \item Set $n_3 = 0, 1, 3, 5$ for a single gauge and compare the 1-in-50-year and
          1-in-100-year return levels from the generated series.
\end{enumerate}

\mkmvalidation

\subsection{Statistical Diagnostics}

The following diagnostics are performed on each generated timeseries:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Autocorrelation function (ACF).} The empirical ACF of the generated
          series should exhibit exponential decay consistent with the specified $\phi$.
          For a non-tidal gauge ($\phi = 0.85$), the lag-1 autocorrelation should be
          approximately 0.85.
    \item \textbf{Seasonal decomposition.} Monthly mean levels should exhibit a clear
          winter peak (January) and summer trough (July), with amplitude proportional to
          $A_s$.
    \item \textbf{Flood frequency.} The number of days exceeding the FloodAlert threshold
          over the 50-year simulation should be consistent with the specified flood
          injection rates: approximately $5 \times \max(n_3, 1)$ minor floods and
          $\max(n_3, 1)$ severe floods.
    \item \textbf{Historical high placement.} If \texttt{HistoricalHighDate} is within the
          simulation window, the generated level on that date must equal
          \texttt{HistoricalHighLevel} exactly.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Cross-Gauge Consistency}

Across the 40-gauge Thames portfolio:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Tidal gauges (downstream, $\lambda > -0.25$) should exhibit higher daily
          variance than non-tidal gauges (upstream, $\lambda \le -0.35$).
    \item The seasonal amplitude ratio $A_s / \sigma_{\text{total}}$ should decrease
          from non-tidal to tidal gauges, reflecting the increasing dominance of
          tidal variability.
    \item All gauges should produce mean levels below their FloodAlert threshold.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Reproducibility Verification}

Given the deterministic seeding (equation~\ref{eq:seed}), regeneration with the same
gauge portfolio must produce byte-identical JSON output. This is verified by the
automated test suite (\texttt{tests/port/gaugehd/}).

\subsection{Backtesting Protocol}

When NRFA real gauge data is available for a station, the synthetic timeseries should be
compared to the observed record:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item Compare empirical monthly mean profiles (synthetic vs.\ observed).
    \item Compare the empirical exceedance frequency at the FloodAlert threshold.
    \item Compute the Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistic between the synthetic and observed
          daily level distributions.
\end{enumerate}

% ===================================================================
% 8. MODEL LIMITATIONS AND KNOWN WEAKNESSES
% ===================================================================
\mkmlimitations

\subsection{Simplified Tidal Model}

The tidal component uses a single spring-neap envelope with uniform random phase, rather
than a proper harmonic decomposition of the tidal constituents (M2, S2, N2, K1, O1, etc.).
This captures the fortnightly amplitude modulation but not the precise timing or asymmetry
of real tidal cycles. For downstream applications that depend on the timing of high water
relative to storm surge, a full tidal harmonic model would be required.

\subsection{No Spatial Correlation}

Each gauge's timeseries is generated independently. In reality, gauges within the same
river reach share common hydrological drivers (rainfall, catchment moisture), producing
strong spatial correlation. The independence assumption means that portfolio-level
statistics computed from the generated series will under-state the co-occurrence of
elevated water levels across multiple gauges.

\subsection{Stationarity}

All parameters are treated as time-invariant over the 50-year simulation. The model does
not account for climate-change-driven trends in mean water levels, seasonal amplitude,
or flood frequency. Users requiring non-stationary analysis should treat the generated
series as representative of current-climate conditions.

\subsection{AR(1) Limitations}

The first-order autoregressive model captures day-to-day persistence but not longer-range
dependence structures such as:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Multi-day flood events (where levels remain elevated for several consecutive
          days due to sustained rainfall).
    \item Long-memory processes (Hurst exponent $> 0.5$) that have been documented in
          some hydrological timeseries.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The AR(1) process produces single-day flood spikes; realistic multi-day flood
events would require a higher-order AR model or a dedicated event model.

\subsection{Uniform Flood Level Distribution}

Flood event levels are drawn from uniform distributions
(equations~\ref{eq:severe_level}~and~\ref{eq:minor_level}), which assigns equal
probability to all levels within the flood range. In reality, lower flood levels are
more common than extreme floods, and a GEV or log-normal distribution would be more
appropriate. The uniform assumption over-states the frequency of the most extreme
flood levels within each severity band.

\subsection{No Baseflow Separation}

The model does not separate baseflow (groundwater-driven) from quickflow
(rainfall-driven) components. For non-tidal gauges where baseflow dominates the
dry-season level, this is acceptable. For catchments with significant groundwater
variability, the constant base level assumption may produce overly smooth summer
levels.

\subsection{Fixed Simulation Length}

The default simulation length is 50 years. For return periods longer than 50 years
(e.g.\ 1-in-100-year events), the generated series provides limited empirical
information and downstream GEV fitting must extrapolate beyond the data range.

\mkmgovernance

\mkmvqitem{1}{Ronseal Test}{Does the model do what it says on the tin?}
Yes. The model generates synthetic daily water level timeseries for flood gauges, producing 50-year histories with seasonal, tidal, and stochastic components calibrated to each gauge's flood thresholds and tidal classification. The generated data is consumed by downstream models (hazard curves, stress testing) as documented. Monthly means, level statistics, and flood exceedance counts are computed as specified.

\mkmvqitem{2}{Accuracy and Stability}{Does the model provide timely, accurate and stable results?}
The model produces deterministic output for a given gauge portfolio (via hash-based seeding). Generated levels respect the specified flood thresholds (base level is 30--50\% of FloodAlert). The AR(1) innovation variance scaling ensures marginal variance is independent of the autocorrelation coefficient. Historical high levels are placed at their exact recorded dates. Results are stable across regeneration cycles.

\mkmvqitem{3}{Resource Usage}{Does the model consume excessive computational resources?}
No. The model uses only the Python standard library with no external dependencies. Generating 50 years of daily data (18,250 records) for a single gauge completes in under 50\,ms. The full 40-gauge Thames portfolio is generated in under 2 seconds. Memory usage is proportional to the number of daily observations (approximately 2\,MB per gauge JSON file).

\mkmvqitem{4}{Override Controls}{Are there controls over manual overrides?}
Model parameters are determined by the tidal classification (derived from longitude) and the gauge portfolio CDM fields. There is no mechanism for manual real-time override of generated water levels. The simulation length (50 years) and tidal classification thresholds are hard-coded constants. Changes require code modification subject to version control.

\mkmvqitem{5}{Output Consumption}{How is the output consumed for risk decision-making?}
The generated timeseries serves three downstream purposes: (i)~monthly means feed the stress model pipeline (\texttt{stressm}) for baseline water levels in stress scenarios; (ii)~the full daily series provides the empirical distribution for GEV hazard curve fitting; (iii)~level statistics (percentiles, annual maxima, flood counts) are displayed in the Trading Desk historical tab and gauge visualisation panels.

\mkmvqitem{6}{Model Chaining}{Is the model's output used as input to another model?}
Yes. The model is an upstream component in the model chain. Its primary downstream consumers are: (a)~the stress model pipeline (MKM-SM-001) which uses monthly means as baseline levels; (b)~the GEV hazard curve builder (MKM-HAZARD-001) which fits exceedance distributions to the generated daily levels; and (c)~the gauge timeseries visualisation. The model's only upstream dependency is the gauge portfolio (\texttt{gauge.json}).

\mkmvqitem{7}{Weaknesses}{Does the model have weaknesses and limitations of use?}
Yes; seven limitations are documented in Section~8. The principal weaknesses are: (i)~simplified tidal model (spring-neap envelope only, no harmonic decomposition); (ii)~no spatial correlation across gauges; (iii)~stationarity assumption (no climate trends); (iv)~AR(1) cannot produce multi-day flood events; (v)~uniform flood level distribution over-states extreme frequencies within each severity band.

\mkmvqitem{8}{Monitoring}{What type of monitoring is required?}
Annual monitoring should include: (a)~comparison of synthetic monthly mean profiles against NRFA observed data where available; (b)~verification that flood exceedance counts are consistent with the gauge portfolio's \texttt{FrequencyExceedLevel3} field; (c)~ACF analysis confirming that the empirical lag-1 autocorrelation matches the specified $\phi$; (d)~cross-gauge consistency checks (tidal variance $>$ non-tidal variance); (e)~reproducibility verification via hash-based seeding.

\mkmvqitem{9}{Overall Risk}{Is the overall model risk acceptable?}
Acceptable. As a Tier~2 model, the GaugeHD synthetic generator does not directly produce a regulated output but provides the historical baseline for Tier~1 downstream models. The key risk---tidal mis-classification distorting the variance structure---is mitigated by the deterministic longitude-based classification (equation~\ref{eq:tidal_class}). The independence assumption across gauges is acceptable because downstream portfolio-level risk aggregation uses separate spatial correlation models. The stationarity limitation is acceptable provided the model is recalibrated when climate projections materially change baseline water levels.
