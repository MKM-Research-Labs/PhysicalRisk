% ===================================================================
% 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
% ===================================================================
\mkmexecsummary

This document presents the mathematical framework, implementation, and validation of the
Storm Intensity Distribution Model, a hybrid probability distribution that combines a
truncated Normal body with a Pareto power-law tail. The model is used to generate
synthetic storm intensity samples for the Physical Risk Swap (PRS) pricing framework
and associated flood-hazard analysis.

The central design objective is to capture two distinct regimes of storm behaviour
simultaneously: (i)~the bulk of observed storm intensities, which are well-described by
a Gaussian distribution calibrated to historical data, and (ii)~the extreme right tail,
where rare but catastrophic events follow heavy-tailed power-law statistics. A smooth
continuity condition at the threshold $u$ ensures the composite exceedance function is
monotonically decreasing and free of discontinuities.

Five pre-calibrated scenario families---\emph{historical}, \emph{baseline},
\emph{moderate\_stress}, \emph{severe\_stress}, and \emph{extreme}---are provided for
regulatory stress testing and internal risk management. Each family specifies a consistent
set of parameters controlling the body and tail of the distribution.

\textbf{Key risk:} The model feeds directly into the hazard curve builder and PRS
pricing engine. An under-estimation of tail heaviness (high $\alpha$) will compress
return-period intensities, leading to under-priced protection contracts. Conversely,
an over-fat tail (low $\alpha$) will over-state extreme event frequency.

% 2. MODEL PURPOSE AND SCOPE

\mkmpurpose

\subsection{Purpose}

The Storm Intensity Distribution Model serves three principal purposes within the
Physical Risk platform:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Synthetic storm generation.} Provide a calibrated probability
          distribution from which storm intensity values (on a 0--100 normalised scale)
          can be sampled via inverse-transform sampling.
    \item \textbf{Return-period calculation.} Map between exceedance probabilities and
          intensity levels, enabling the computation of $T$-year return-period intensities
          for any desired horizon.
    \item \textbf{Stress-scenario support.} Offer a parameterised family of distributions
          that allows risk managers and regulators to explore the sensitivity of downstream
          outputs (hazard curves, PRS spreads) to changes in storm climatology.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{In scope:} Univariate storm intensity distribution; exceedance
          probability calculation; inverse exceedance (quantile) function; Monte Carlo
          sampling; return-period mapping; scenario family calibration.
    \item \textbf{Out of scope:} Spatial correlation of storm intensity across gauges;
          temporal clustering of events; wind-field modelling; rainfall disaggregation;
          the downstream gauge response and flood depth models.
    \item \textbf{Upstream dependencies:} Historical storm catalogue (for parameter
          calibration); scenario specifications from risk management.
    \item \textbf{Downstream consumers:} Storm--gauge forward model
          (\texttt{storm\_gauge\_model}); hazard curve builder; PRS pricing engine.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Regulatory Context}

This model is classified as a \emph{Tier~2} component under the internal model
governance framework. It does not directly produce a regulated output but is a
material input to the hazard curve and PRS pricing models, both of which are
Tier~1. Accordingly, it is subject to annual independent validation and parameter
review.

% ===================================================================
% 3. MATHEMATICAL FRAMEWORK
% ===================================================================
\mkmframework

\subsection{Overview}

The Storm Intensity Distribution is a piecewise-continuous probability distribution
defined on the interval $[I_{\min},\, I_{\max}]$. It comprises two regions:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Body} ($I_{\min} \le x \le u$): a truncated Normal distribution
          $\mathcal{N}(\mu,\,\sigma^2)$.
    \item \textbf{Tail} ($x > u$): a Pareto power-law with shape parameter $\alpha$
          and scale parameter $u$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent where $u$ is the tail threshold, $\mu$ is the base mean, $\sigma$ is the
base standard deviation, and $\alpha$ is the tail index.

\subsection{Normal Body}

\subsubsection{Probability Density Function}

The standard Normal PDF is:
\begin{equation}
    \phi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\,z^2\right)
    \label{eq:normal_pdf}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $z = (x - \mu)/\sigma$ is the standardised intensity.

\subsubsection{Cumulative Distribution Function}

The standard Normal CDF is implemented via the error function:
\begin{equation}
    \Phi(z) = \frac{1}{2}\left[1 + \operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right]
    \label{eq:normal_cdf}
\end{equation}

\noindent The implementation uses the platform's built-in \texttt{math.erf} function,
avoiding any dependency on external numerical libraries such as SciPy. This design
choice ensures portability across deployment environments.

\subsubsection{Exceedance Probability (Normal Region)}

For intensity $x \le u$, the exceedance probability under the Normal body is:
\begin{equation}
    S_N(x) = \Pr(X > x) = 1 - \Phi\!\left(\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right)
    \label{eq:normal_exceedance}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Pareto Tail}

For intensity values above the threshold $u$, the exceedance function follows a
Pareto power law:
\begin{equation}
    S_P(x) = \left(\frac{u}{x}\right)^{\!\alpha}, \quad x \ge u
    \label{eq:pareto_exceedance}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\alpha > 0$ is the tail index (also called the shape parameter).
The tail index controls the heaviness of the upper tail:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item $\alpha < 2$: very heavy (``fat'') tail --- extreme events are relatively
          frequent;
    \item $2 \le \alpha < 3$: moderate tail;
    \item $\alpha \ge 3$: thin tail --- extreme events are rare.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Note that for $\alpha \le 1$ the distribution has infinite mean, and for
$\alpha \le 2$ it has infinite variance. In the implemented scenario families,
$\alpha$ ranges from $1.2$ (extreme stress) to $3.0$ (historical), ensuring that
at least the mean is always finite.

\subsection{Continuity Condition}

The composite exceedance function must be continuous at the threshold $u$. Define
the \emph{tail probability} as the probability, under the base Normal, of exceeding
the threshold:
\begin{equation}
    p_{\text{tail}} = S_N(u) = 1 - \Phi\!\left(\frac{u - \mu}{\sigma}\right)
    \label{eq:tail_prob}
\end{equation}

The composite exceedance function is then:
\begin{equation}
    S(x) = \Pr(X > x) =
    \begin{cases}
        1 & \text{if } x \le I_{\min} \\[4pt]
        S_N(x) & \text{if } I_{\min} < x \le u \\[4pt]
        p_{\text{tail}} \cdot S_P(x) = p_{\text{tail}} \cdot \left(\dfrac{u}{x}\right)^{\!\alpha}
            & \text{if } x > u \\[4pt]
        0 & \text{if } x \ge I_{\max}
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:composite_exceedance}
\end{equation}

\noindent At $x = u$, the Pareto branch gives $S_P(u) = (u/u)^\alpha = 1$, so
$S(u) = p_{\text{tail}} \cdot 1 = p_{\text{tail}} = S_N(u)$. The function is
therefore continuous.

\subsection{Inverse Exceedance (Quantile Function)}

Sampling and return-period calculations require the inverse of $S(x)$. Given a
target exceedance probability $p \in (0,1)$:

\begin{equation}
    S^{-1}(p) =
    \begin{cases}
        \mu + \sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(1 - p)
            & \text{if } p \ge p_{\text{tail}} \\[6pt]
        u \cdot \left(\dfrac{p_{\text{tail}}}{p}\right)^{\!1/\alpha}
            & \text{if } p < p_{\text{tail}}
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:inverse_exceedance}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\Phi^{-1}$ is the inverse standard Normal CDF. The implementation
uses a rational approximation (Peter Acklam's algorithm) to compute $\Phi^{-1}$
without external dependencies. The approximation is accurate to approximately
$1.15 \times 10^{-9}$ in absolute error across the full domain.

The result is clamped to the interval $[I_{\min},\, I_{\max}]$:
\begin{equation}
    Q(p) = \max\!\Big(I_{\min},\;\min\!\big(I_{\max},\; S^{-1}(p)\big)\Big)
    \label{eq:clamped_quantile}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Return-Period Intensity}

The intensity associated with a return period of $T$ years, given an average of
$\lambda$ storms per year, is obtained by setting the per-storm exceedance
probability to $(T\lambda)^{-1}$:
\begin{equation}
    I_T = S^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1}{T\,\lambda}\right)
    \label{eq:return_period}
\end{equation}

\noindent The default assumption is $\lambda = 20$ storms per year. For example,
the 1-in-100-year intensity corresponds to the exceedance probability
$p = 1/(100 \times 20) = 0.0005$.

\subsection{Sampling Procedure}

Samples are drawn via inverse-transform sampling with additive jitter:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item Draw $U \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$.
    \item Compute the base intensity $x_0 = S^{-1}(U)$ using equation~\eqref{eq:inverse_exceedance}.
    \item Add Gaussian jitter: $x = x_0 + \varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\, 0.5^2)$.
    \item Clamp: $x \leftarrow \max(I_{\min},\, \min(I_{\max},\, x))$.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The small jitter ($\sigma_\varepsilon = 0.5$) prevents discrete artefacts
in the sampled distribution without materially altering the distributional shape.

\subsection{Summary Statistics}

Summary statistics are computed via Monte Carlo simulation with $n = 10{,}000$
samples (configurable). The following quantities are reported:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Mean and standard deviation.
    \item Percentiles: $p_{10}$, $p_{25}$, $p_{50}$ (median), $p_{75}$, $p_{90}$, $p_{95}$, $p_{99}$.
    \item Exceedance probabilities: $\Pr(X > 60)$, $\Pr(X > 70)$, $\Pr(X > 80)$, $\Pr(X > 90)$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The percentile function uses simple order-statistic indexing:
$\hat{q}_p = x_{(\lfloor np/100 \rfloor)}$ on the sorted sample.

\subsection{Calibration Approach}

Parameters for the \texttt{historical} scenario are calibrated to the historical
storm catalogue by maximum likelihood estimation on the body and Hill estimation
on the tail. The remaining scenarios are constructed by expert judgement, shifting
the four key parameters along the directions described above to produce progressively
more stressed distributions. The calibration procedure is reviewed annually as
part of the model governance cycle.

\mkmimplementation

\subsection{Software Architecture}

The model is implemented in Python 3 within the \texttt{src/models/intensity/}
package, comprising three modules:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \texttt{parameters.py} --- \texttt{DistributionParameters} dataclass and
          the \texttt{SCENARIO\_FAMILIES} dictionary.
    \item \texttt{distribution.py} --- \texttt{IntensityDistribution} class containing
          all mathematical logic.
    \item \texttt{cli.py} --- command-line interface and scenario comparison utilities.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dependency-Free Design}

A deliberate design decision was made to implement the model using only the Python
standard library (\texttt{math}, \texttt{random}). Specifically:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item The Normal CDF is computed via \texttt{math.erf} (equation~\ref{eq:normal_cdf}).
    \item The inverse Normal CDF uses Peter Acklam's rational approximation, which
          provides full double-precision accuracy without requiring SciPy.
    \item Random sampling uses \texttt{random.random()} and \texttt{random.gauss()}.
\end{itemize}

\noindent This eliminates deployment issues in constrained environments (e.g.\
containerised microservices, serverless functions) where installing NumPy or SciPy
may not be practical.

\subsection{Inverse Normal CDF Approximation}

The Acklam approximation partitions the unit interval into three regions:
\begin{equation}
    \Phi^{-1}(p) \approx
    \begin{cases}
        \dfrac{c_0 q + c_1 q^2 + \cdots + c_5 q^5}{d_0 q + d_1 q^2 + \cdots + d_3 q^3 + 1}
            & \text{if } p < 0.02425, \; q = \sqrt{-2\ln p} \\[10pt]
        \dfrac{a_0 r + a_1 r^2 + \cdots + a_5 r^5}{b_0 r + b_1 r^2 + \cdots + b_4 r^4 + 1} \cdot q
            & \text{if } 0.02425 \le p \le 0.97575, \; q = p - 0.5,\; r = q^2 \\[10pt]
        -\dfrac{c_0 q + \cdots + c_5 q^5}{d_0 q + \cdots + d_3 q^3 + 1}
            & \text{if } p > 0.97575, \; q = \sqrt{-2\ln(1-p)}
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:acklam}
\end{equation}

\noindent The six $a$-coefficients, five $b$-coefficients, six $c$-coefficients, and
four $d$-coefficients are hard-coded constants derived by Acklam. Maximum absolute
error is approximately $1.15 \times 10^{-9}$.

\subsection{Numerical Considerations}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Clamping.} All outputs are clamped to $[I_{\min},\, I_{\max}]$ to
          prevent physically meaningless intensity values.
    \item \textbf{Edge cases.} $S^{-1}(0) = I_{\max}$ and $S^{-1}(1) = I_{\min}$
          by convention.
    \item \textbf{Jitter in sampling.} The additive Gaussian jitter
          ($\sigma_\varepsilon = 0.5$) is small relative to $\sigma$ and does not
          materially affect the distributional moments. It smooths the empirical
          histogram of sampled values.
    \item \textbf{Pre-computation.} The tail probability $p_{\text{tail}}$ is computed
          once at initialisation and cached, since it depends only on the fixed
          parameters $\mu$, $\sigma$, and $u$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Serialisation}

The \texttt{to\_dict()} method serialises the full distribution state, including
both the parameter set and the pre-computed tail probability, to a JSON-compatible
dictionary. This supports audit trails and model versioning.

\mkmsensitivity

The model has four primary sensitivity dimensions: the tail index $\alpha$, the
tail threshold $u$, the body standard deviation $\sigma$, and the body mean $\mu$.

\input{sensitivity_tables}

\subsection{Sensitivity to Tail Index ($\alpha$)}

The tail index is the single most influential parameter for extreme-event
frequency. Its effect on the $T$-year return-period intensity $I_T$ can be seen
by differentiating equation~\eqref{eq:inverse_exceedance} in the tail region:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial I_T}{\partial \alpha}
    = -\frac{I_T}{\alpha^2}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{p_{\text{tail}}}{p}\right)
    < 0
    \label{eq:sensitivity_alpha}
\end{equation}

\noindent Since $p < p_{\text{tail}}$ in the tail, the logarithm is positive, so
$\partial I_T / \partial \alpha < 0$: increasing $\alpha$ (thinner tail) \emph{decreases}
return-period intensities. Key observations:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Moving from $\alpha = 3.0$ (historical) to $\alpha = 1.5$ (severe stress)
          approximately doubles the 1-in-100-year intensity.
    \item For $\alpha < 2$, the Pareto distribution has infinite variance, amplifying
          sampling uncertainty in Monte Carlo estimates.
    \item The sensitivity is non-linear: changes near $\alpha = 1$ have a far greater
          effect than changes near $\alpha = 3$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Sensitivity to Tail Threshold ($u$)}

The threshold $u$ controls where the Pareto tail engages. Lowering $u$ has two
compounding effects:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item It increases $p_{\text{tail}} = S_N(u)$, enlarging the fraction of the
          distribution governed by the power law.
    \item It provides a lower ``base'' from which the Pareto scaling amplifies
          intensities.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Moving $u$ from 60 to 45 (historical $\to$ extreme) substantially
increases the probability of events above intensity 80. Validators should note that
$u$ and $\alpha$ are partially confounded: a low $u$ with moderate $\alpha$ can
produce similar tail behaviour to a high $u$ with low $\alpha$.

\subsection{Sensitivity to Body Standard Deviation ($\sigma$)}

The standard deviation controls the spread of the Normal body and indirectly affects
$p_{\text{tail}}$. Increasing $\sigma$ with $u$ fixed increases the tail probability
because more mass falls above the threshold. For the baseline parameters:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item $\sigma = 12$: $p_{\text{tail}} \approx 4.8\%$
    \item $\sigma = 15$: $p_{\text{tail}} \approx 15.9\%$
    \item $\sigma = 20$: $p_{\text{tail}} \approx 22.7\%$ (with $u=45$, extreme scenario)
\end{itemize}

\noindent The interaction between $\sigma$ and $u$ means that validation should
examine joint sensitivity rather than one-at-a-time perturbation.

\subsection{Sensitivity to Body Mean ($\mu$)}

Shifting $\mu$ upward translates the entire body rightward, increasing both the
median intensity and $p_{\text{tail}}$. This is the primary mechanism by which the
stress scenarios increase average storm severity.

\subsection{Recommended Sensitivity Tests}

For bank validation purposes, the following sensitivity tests are recommended:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item Vary $\alpha$ from 1.0 to 4.0 in steps of 0.25, holding other parameters
          at baseline. Report 1-in-50 and 1-in-100 year intensities.
    \item Vary $u$ from 40 to 70 in steps of 5, holding $\alpha = 2.5$. Report
          $\Pr(X > 80)$.
    \item Jointly vary $\alpha$ and $u$ on a $5 \times 5$ grid. Report the
          1-in-100 year intensity surface.
    \item Run 50 independent 10{,}000-sample Monte Carlo runs for each scenario
          and report the coefficient of variation of the $p_{99}$ estimate.
\end{enumerate}

\mkmvalidation

\subsection{Historical Comparison}

The primary validation approach is comparison of the fitted distribution against
the observed storm intensity catalogue. The following diagnostics are performed:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Quantile--Quantile (QQ) plots.} Theoretical quantiles from
          $S^{-1}(p)$ are plotted against empirical quantiles from the sorted
          historical sample. Departures from the 1:1 line indicate model misfit.
    \item \textbf{Probability--Probability (PP) plots.} Empirical exceedance
          probabilities $\hat{S}(x_i) = (n - i + 0.5)/n$ are plotted against
          theoretical $S(x_i)$.
    \item \textbf{Kolmogorov--Smirnov (KS) test.} The maximum absolute difference
          $D_n = \sup_x |S_n(x) - S(x)|$ is computed and compared to the critical
          value at the 5\% significance level.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Tail Behaviour Analysis}

The Pareto tail is validated separately:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Hill plot.} The Hill estimator $\hat{\alpha}_k$ is plotted as a
          function of the number of upper order statistics $k$. A stable plateau
          confirms the appropriateness of the Pareto model and provides an
          empirical estimate of $\alpha$.
    \item \textbf{Mean excess plot.} The sample mean excess function
          $e_n(u) = \frac{1}{n_u}\sum_{i: x_i > u}(x_i - u)$ is plotted against $u$.
          A linear mean excess function is consistent with a Pareto tail.
    \item \textbf{Log--log exceedance plot.} $\log S(x)$ vs.\ $\log x$ should be
          approximately linear for $x > u$, with slope $-\alpha$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scenario Consistency}

Each pre-defined scenario family is validated for internal consistency:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item The exceedance function $S(x)$ must be monotonically decreasing. This is
          guaranteed by construction (Section~\ref{eq:composite_exceedance}).
    \item Return-period intensities must be monotonically increasing in $T$:
          $I_{T_1} < I_{T_2}$ for $T_1 < T_2$.
    \item Stress scenarios must produce higher return-period intensities than the
          baseline for all return periods, confirming that the scenario ladder is
          coherent.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Monte Carlo Convergence}

The Monte Carlo summary statistics (mean, percentiles, exceedance probabilities)
are subject to sampling noise. Convergence is assessed by:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Running repeated simulations with different seeds and computing the
          coefficient of variation (CV) of each statistic.
    \item Verifying that with $n = 10{,}000$ samples, the CV of the mean is below
          1\% and the CV of $p_{99}$ is below 10\%.
    \item For tail-sensitive outputs ($p_{99}$, exceedance probabilities above 80),
          larger samples ($n = 100{,}000$) may be required for stable estimates.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Backtesting Protocol}

On an annual basis, the most recent year of observed storm data is compared to the
model's predictions:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item Compute the empirical exceedance frequencies for thresholds 60, 70, 80, 90.
    \item Compare to the model's theoretical exceedance probabilities
          $S(60)$, $S(70)$, $S(80)$, $S(90)$.
    \item Flag any threshold where the observed frequency exceeds the 95\%
          confidence bound derived from the binomial distribution.
\end{enumerate}

% ===================================================================
% 8. MODEL LIMITATIONS AND KNOWN WEAKNESSES
% ===================================================================
\mkmlimitations

\subsection{Independence Assumption}

The model treats each storm intensity as an independent draw from the distribution.
In reality, storm intensities may exhibit:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Temporal clustering.} Severe storm seasons may produce multiple
          high-intensity events in rapid succession (e.g.\ during active atmospheric
          patterns).
    \item \textbf{Serial correlation.} Consecutive storms may share synoptic-scale
          drivers, inducing positive autocorrelation in intensity sequences.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The independence assumption means that the model may under-estimate
the probability of multiple extreme events occurring within a single year,
which has implications for aggregate annual loss estimates.

\subsection{Stationarity}

All parameters ($\mu$, $\sigma$, $u$, $\alpha$) are treated as time-invariant
constants within each scenario. The model does not account for:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Climate change trends.} Long-term shifts in storm intensity driven
          by rising sea-surface temperatures, changes in atmospheric circulation, or
          altered precipitation patterns.
    \item \textbf{Decadal variability.} Multi-year oscillations (e.g.\ ENSO, NAO,
          AMO) that modulate storm frequency and intensity.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Users requiring non-stationary analysis should use the scenario families
as discrete snapshots of different climate states rather than interpolating between
them.

\subsection{No Spatial Correlation}

The model is univariate and does not capture spatial dependence of storm intensity
across multiple gauge locations. When the same storm affects multiple gauges, the
intensities experienced at each gauge are correlated. Sampling independently from
this distribution for each gauge will under-estimate portfolio-level tail risk
due to diversification effects that do not exist in reality.

\subsection{Truncation and Clamping}

Clamping to $[I_{\min},\, I_{\max}]$ introduces a hard boundary that assigns
zero probability to intensities outside this range. This is physically motivated
($I_{\min} = 10$ represents a minimum detectable storm; $I_{\max} = 100$ is the
scale ceiling) but creates a slight probability mass distortion near the boundaries.

\subsection{Sampling Jitter}

The additive Gaussian jitter ($\sigma_\varepsilon = 0.5$) marginally alters the
theoretical distribution, effectively convolving it with a narrow Gaussian kernel.
While this improves the visual quality of sampled histograms, it means that the
empirical moments of large samples will not converge exactly to the theoretical
moments of the piecewise distribution. The effect is negligible for practical
sample sizes.

\subsection{Threshold Selection}

The choice of $u$ is a modelling judgement that determines the boundary between
``normal'' and ``extreme'' behaviour. If $u$ is set too low, the Pareto tail
governs too large a fraction of the distribution, potentially over-stating
extreme risk. If $u$ is set too high, the tail has too little probability mass
to influence return-period calculations. There is no automatic threshold
selection mechanism; $u$ is set by expert judgement for each scenario.

\subsection{Single-Peril Limitation}

The model captures storm intensity as a univariate scalar. It does not decompose
intensity into constituent hazard components (wind speed, rainfall accumulation,
storm surge) that may have different distributional characteristics. Multi-peril
extensions would require a multivariate framework.

% ===================================================================
% 9. GOVERNANCE VALIDATION QUESTIONS
% ===================================================================
\mkmgovernance

\mkmvqitem{1}{Ronseal Test}{Does the model do what it says on the tin?}
Yes. The model provides a calibrated hybrid Normal-Pareto probability distribution for storm intensity, delivering: (i)~exceedance probability calculations for arbitrary thresholds, (ii)~inverse exceedance (quantile) function for return-period mapping, (iii)~Monte Carlo sampling via inverse-transform, and (iv)~five pre-calibrated scenario families for stress testing. All documented outputs are produced and consumed by downstream models.

\mkmvqitem{2}{Accuracy and Stability}{Does the model provide timely, accurate and stable results?}
The historical scenario parameters are calibrated to the storm catalogue via MLE (body) and Hill estimation (tail). The composite exceedance function is continuous by construction (Section~3.4). Monte Carlo summary statistics (mean, percentiles) converge to within 1\% CV at $n = 10{,}000$ samples. The Acklam inverse Normal CDF approximation is accurate to $1.15 \times 10^{-9}$. Results are fully deterministic when a random seed is specified.

\mkmvqitem{3}{Resource Usage}{Does the model consume excessive computational resources?}
No. The model is implemented using only the Python standard library (\texttt{math}, \texttt{random}) with no external dependencies. Generating 10,000 samples completes in under 100\,ms. The distribution parameters and tail probability are pre-computed at initialisation ($O(1)$). Exceedance probability and quantile evaluations are $O(1)$ per call. Memory usage is negligible.

\mkmvqitem{4}{Override Controls}{Are there controls over manual overrides?}
Distribution parameters are set via named scenario families (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}) or explicit constructor arguments. No mechanism exists for manual real-time override of computed exceedance probabilities or sampled values. Changes to scenario parameters require code modification and are subject to version control. The \texttt{to\_dict()} serialisation method captures the full parameter state for audit trail purposes.

\mkmvqitem{5}{Output Consumption}{How is the output consumed for risk decision-making?}
Sampled intensity values feed directly into the Storm-Gauge Forward Model (MKM-STORM-002), which converts them to gauge water levels. Return-period intensities are used in stress testing scenarios presented to the MRC. Exceedance probabilities inform the scenario selection for regulatory stress tests. The five scenario families support structured what-if analysis for climate change sensitivity assessments.

\mkmvqitem{6}{Model Chaining}{Is the model's output used as input to another model?}
Yes. The model is an upstream component in the model chain. Its primary downstream consumer is the Storm-Gauge Forward Model (MKM-STORM-002), which converts intensity samples to water level responses. These in turn feed the GEV Hazard Curve Model (MKM-HAZARD-001) and subsequently the PRS Pricing Engine (MKM-PRS-001). The model has no upstream model dependencies; it is parameterised from the historical storm catalogue and expert scenario specifications.

\mkmvqitem{7}{Weaknesses}{Does the model have weaknesses and limitations of use?}
Yes; seven limitations are documented in Section~8. The principal weaknesses are: (i)~independence assumption (no temporal clustering or serial correlation); (ii)~stationarity (no climate change trends or decadal variability); (iii)~univariate scalar intensity (no decomposition into wind, rainfall, surge components); (iv)~no spatial correlation across gauges; and (v)~threshold selection by expert judgement rather than automatic methods.

\mkmvqitem{8}{Monitoring}{What type of monitoring is required?}
Annual monitoring should include: (a)~recalibration of the historical scenario against the most recent year of storm observations; (b)~Hill plot stability review to confirm the appropriateness of the Pareto tail; (c)~KS test of the fitted distribution against empirical data; (d)~comparison of 1-in-50 and 1-in-100 year return-period intensities against the previous year's estimates, with a 10\% drift threshold; (e)~backtesting of exceedance frequencies at thresholds 60, 70, 80, and 90 against observed counts.

\mkmvqitem{9}{Overall Risk}{Is the overall model risk acceptable?}
Acceptable. As a Tier~2 model, the Storm Intensity Distribution does not directly produce a regulated output but is a material input to Tier~1 downstream models. The key risk---tail index misspecification---is mitigated by five pre-calibrated scenarios spanning thin to very fat tails. The dependency-free implementation minimises deployment risk. The stationarity limitation is acceptable provided the scenario families are periodically recalibrated to reflect evolving climate conditions.
