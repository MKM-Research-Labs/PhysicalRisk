% 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
% ===================================================================
\mkmexecsummary

This document presents the mathematical framework, implementation, and validation of the
Storm Sequence Generator (MKM-SS-001), a significant upgrade from single-event storm
simulation to \textbf{multi-storm sequence modelling} as the primary method of synthetic
event generation within the Physical Risk platform.

Version~2.0 replaces the single-storm paradigm (one intensity sample $\to$ one
water-level response) with the \emph{insurance hours clause} model: a group of 1--5
related precipitation events, collectively referred to as a \emph{StormSequence}, all
fitting within a \textbf{168-hour (7-day) event window}. All precipitation is required
to end by hour~156, leaving a minimum 12-hour drainage observation window. This design
directly mirrors the \emph{loss occurrence period} clause used in catastrophe reinsurance
and parameterised cat-bond structures.

Four sequence types are defined, ordered by inter-storm coupling:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Isolated} --- a single precipitation event (1~storm).
  \item \textbf{Doublet} --- two events with a medium recovery gap (24--72\,h).
  \item \textbf{Cluster} --- 3--4 events with medium gaps; typical of active frontal passages.
  \item \textbf{Persistent} --- 4--5 events with short gaps (6--36\,h); modelling sustained
        blocking-pattern precipitation.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The probability of a multi-storm sequence increases with intensity category:
5\% (minimal) to 85\% (catastrophic), reflecting the meteorological tendency for the most
severe atmospheric systems to produce extended sequences of precipitation events.

\textbf{Key design principle:} All sequences are guaranteed to fit within the event window
without rejection sampling or fallback logic. Storm durations are budgeted as
$d_{\max} = 156 / n$ per storm, and any sampled gaps that would overflow the remaining
window are scaled proportionally. This guarantees correctness in $O(1)$ and produces
zero ``could not fit'' warnings at any scale.

\textbf{Downstream impact:} The multi-storm framework trains a significantly more capable
flood probability classifier (MKM-FC-001 v2.0). With 10{,}000 sequences providing richer
multi-peak hydrograph shapes and \textbf{ten times the training data} (1{,}680{,}000
vectors per gauge vs.\ 168{,}000 in v1.x), the stressm classifier achieves a
\textbf{mean AUC-ROC of $\approx$0.993} across all Thames gauges, a further 4\%
improvement over the already-exceptional v1.3 result.

\mkmpurpose

\subsection{Purpose}
The Storm Sequence Generator serves three principal purposes within the Physical Risk
platform:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Synthetic sequence generation.} Produce a catalogue of 10{,}000
        StormSequence objects covering the full intensity spectrum (moderate through
        catastrophic), each containing 1--5 temporally structured precipitation events.
  \item \textbf{Hydrograph composition.} Provide temporal structure (storm start times,
        durations, inter-storm gaps, peak positions) so the Storm-Gauge Forward Model
        can compose multi-peak hydrographs showing catchment recovery between events.
  \item \textbf{Classifier training data.} Feed the stressm flood probability classifier
        (MKM-FC-001 v2.0) with training sequences that include antecedent-wetness dynamics:
        a gauge that partially recovered from Storm~1 is at elevated risk from Storm~2.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{In scope:} Sequence type selection; storm count and duration sampling;
        inter-storm gap sampling and budget-scaling; intensity correlation within sequences;
        precipitation calculation; event window validation; batch generation across all
        intensity categories.
  \item \textbf{Out of scope:} Spatial footprint and track geometry (handled by
        MKM-SG-001); GEV hazard curve fitting (MKM-GH-001); PRS pricing (MKM-PR-001);
        climate change projections; tidal interactions.
  \item \textbf{Upstream dependencies:} Intensity category weights and base intensity
        parameters (configured in \texttt{batch\_generator.py}).
  \item \textbf{Downstream consumers:} stressm flood classifier training pipeline
        (\texttt{src/port/src/stressm.py}); portfolio stress test backend
        (\texttt{src/routes/trading/port\_stress.py}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Model Chain Position}
The sequence generator sits between intensity parameterisation and gauge-level response:
\[
\text{Intensity Params}
\xrightarrow{\text{batch\_generator}}
\textbf{StormSequence (MKM-SS-001)}

the Portfolio Stress P\&L calculation displayed to trading desk risk managers.

\mkmframework

\subsection{Event Window and Timing Constraints}

The insurance \emph{hours clause} defines a 168-hour (7-day) event window $W$:

\begin{equation}
  W = 168\,\text{h}
  \label{eq:event_window}
\end{equation}

All precipitation within a sequence must end by hour $W_P$:

\begin{equation}
  W_P = W - W_{\text{drain}} = 168 - 12 = 156\,\text{h}
  \label{eq:precip_window}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $W_{\text{drain}} = 12\,\text{h}$ is the minimum drainage observation
window required by the contract specification. The drainage window for a specific sequence
is the time from the end of the last storm to the end of the event window:

\begin{equation}
  W_{\text{drain}}^{(\text{seq})} = W - \left(t_{\text{start},n}
    + d_n\right)
  \label{eq:seq_drain}
\end{equation}

where $t_{\text{start},n}$ and $d_n$ are the start time and duration of the last
(index $n$) storm. Validity requires $W_{\text{drain}}^{(\text{seq})} \geq W_{\text{drain}} = 12\,\text{h}$.

\subsection{Sequence Type Selection}

For each event, the generator first decides whether to produce a multi-storm sequence
at all, with probability depending on the intensity category:

\begin{equation}
  P(\text{sequence}) =
  \begin{cases}
    0.05 & \text{minimal} \\
    0.15 & \text{baseline} \\
    0.30 & \text{moderate} \\
    0.50 & \text{severe} \\
    0.70 & \text{extreme} \\
    0.85 & \text{catastrophic}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:seq_prob}
\end{equation}

distribution with weights that shift toward more coupled types as intensity increases

\subsubsection{Budget Cap}
\label{sec:budget_cap}

To guarantee that all storms in a sequence fit within $W_P = 156\,\text{h}$, each
sampled duration is capped to a per-storm budget:

\begin{equation}
  d_i = \min\!\left(D_i,\; \frac{W_P}{n}\right), \quad i = 1, \ldots, n
  \label{eq:duration_cap}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $n$ is the number of storms in the sequence. This is the key
invariant of v2.0: \textbf{storm durations are bounded before gap sampling}, so
the remaining budget for gaps is always non-negative.

\noindent The per-storm cap as a function of sequence size:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Isolated ($n=1$): cap = 156\,h --- effectively unconstrained for all categories.
  \item Doublet ($n=2$): cap = 78\,h.
  \item Cluster ($n=3$): cap = 52\,h; ($n=4$): cap = 39\,h.
  \item Persistent ($n=4$): cap = 39\,h; ($n=5$): cap = 31.2\,h.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Note that for catastrophic sequences (mode = 120\,h, max = 240\,h), the cap
is active for all cluster/persistent configurations, clamping what would otherwise be
multi-day storms into the available budget. This is physically appropriate: extreme
storms in rapid succession are each necessarily shorter-lived.

\subsection{Inter-Storm Gap Sampling}
\label{sec:gap}

Gaps are sampled from triangular distributions whose type depends on the sequence type
(Table~\ref{tab:gap_params}). SHORT gaps model active blocking patterns; MEDIUM gaps
model partial catchment recovery between frontal passages.

  \label{tab:gap_params}
  \begin{tabular}{llrrrl}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Type} & \textbf{Used by} & \textbf{Min} & \textbf{Mode} & \textbf{Max}
      & \textbf{Physical interpretation} \\
    \midrule
    SHORT  & persistent & 6 & 18 & 36 & Minimal drainage; high compounding \\
    MEDIUM & doublet, cluster & 24 & 48 & 72 & Partial drainage (30--60\% recovery) \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsubsection{Gap Budget Scaling}
\label{sec:gap_scale}

Let $R = W_P - \sum_{i=1}^{n} d_i$ denote the remaining time budget after placing all
$n$ storms, and let $G_1, \ldots, G_{n-1}$ be raw gap samples from
Equation~(\ref{eq:duration_sample}). If the total raw gap exceeds the budget:

\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} G_j > R
  \label{eq:gap_overflow}
\end{equation}

\noindent then all gaps are scaled proportionally:

\begin{equation}
  g_j = G_j \cdot \frac{R}{\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} G_k}, \quad j = 1, \ldots, n-1
  \label{eq:gap_scale}
\end{equation}

\noindent This preserves the relative spacing between storms (doublet gaps remain larger
than persistent gaps) while guaranteeing the timing constraint. If $\sum G_j \leq R$,
the raw samples are used unmodified. The combined guarantee from
Equations~(\ref{eq:duration_cap}) and~(\ref{eq:gap_scale}) ensures that
$\sum d_i + \sum g_j \leq W_P$ holds in all cases.

\subsection{Storm Placement}
\label{sec:placement}

Storm start times are computed sequentially from the placed durations and gaps:

\begin{align}
  t_{\text{start},1} &= 0 \label{eq:start_first} \\
  t_{\text{start},i} &= t_{\text{start},i-1} + d_{i-1} + g_{i-1},
    \quad i = 2, \ldots, n \label{eq:start_i}
\end{align}

\noindent The last storm ends at $t_{\text{start},n} + d_n \leq W_P$, verified by the
post-generation validator (Section~\ref{sec:validation}).

\subsection{Intensity Sampling Within Sequences}
\label{sec:intensity}

The base intensity $F_0$ for the sequence is drawn from a Normal distribution
per intensity category (Table~\ref{tab:intensity_params}), floored at~0.1:

\begin{equation}
  F_0 \sim \max\!\left(0.1,\; \mathcal{N}(\mu_{\text{cat}},\; \sigma_{\text{cat}}^2)\right)
  \label{eq:base_intensity}
\end{equation}

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{Base intensity distribution parameters by category.}
  \label{tab:intensity_params}
  \begin{tabular}{lrr}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Category} & $\mu$ (mean) & $\sigma$ (std) \\
    \midrule
    minimal      & 0.30 & 0.10 \\
    baseline     & 0.60 & 0.15 \\
    moderate     & 1.00 & 0.20 \\
    severe       & 1.80 & 0.30 \\
    extreme      & 3.00 & 0.50 \\
    catastrophic & 5.00 & 0.80 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The individual storm intensities are drawn as correlated perturbations around $F_0$
using the following asymmetric scheme. Let $\delta \in [-0.2, +0.2]$ be the
variation range ($\pm 20\%$). For the first storm ($i=0$):

\begin{equation}
  F_0^{(i=0)} = \max\!\left(0.1,\; F_0 \cdot \left(1 + \delta_0\right)\right),
  \quad \delta_0 \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(-0.2,\; 0)
  \label{eq:first_storm_intensity}
\end{equation}

\noindent The first storm is always drawn with a downward variation (0 to $-20\%$)
from the base, reflecting that the triggering event may be somewhat weaker than the
sequence peak.

For subsequent storms ($i \geq 1$):

\begin{equation}
  F_0^{(i)} = \max\!\left(0.1,\; F_0 \cdot \left(1 + \delta_i\right)\right),
  \quad \delta_i \sim
  \begin{cases}
    \mathrm{Uniform}(0,\; +0.2) & \text{with probability } 0.70 \\
    \mathrm{Uniform}(-0.2,\; 0) & \text{with probability } 0.30
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:later_storm_intensity}
\end{equation}

\noindent The 70\% probability of maintained or increasing intensity models the
antecedent soil moisture mechanism: catchments partially saturated by Storm~$i{-}1$
respond more severely to Storm~$i$ even if raw precipitation is similar.

\subsection{Precipitation Calculation}

The precipitation (mm) for storm $i$ is:

\begin{equation}
  P_i = P_{\text{base}} \cdot F_0^{(i)}
  \label{eq:precipitation}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $P_{\text{base}}$ is a catchment-specific baseline precipitation. For
the Thames catchment, $P_{\text{base}} = 35\,\text{mm}$. The peak position within the
storm (dimensionless, $[0,1]$) is drawn from a symmetric Beta distribution:

\begin{equation}
  \text{peak\_position} \sim \mathrm{Beta}(2, 2)
  \label{eq:peak_pos}
\end{equation}

\noindent This produces a bell-shaped distribution centred at 0.5, favouring
mid-storm peaks while allowing early and late peaks.

\subsection{Aggregate Sequence Statistics}

The following aggregate statistics are computed for each StormSequence:

\begin{align}
  P_{\text{total}} &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} P_i \label{eq:total_precip} \\
  F_{\max} &= \max_{i}\, F_0^{(i)} \label{eq:max_intensity} \\
  \bar{F} &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} F_0^{(i)} \label{eq:avg_intensity} \\
  F_{\text{cum}} &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} F_0^{(i)} \label{eq:cum_intensity} \\
  D_{\text{total}} &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} d_i + \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} g_j \label{eq:total_duration}
\end{align}

\noindent $F_{\text{cum}}$ is particularly important for portfolio stress testing: it
captures the compounding effect of multiple storms rather than just the single peak.

\subsection{Generation Flow}

The primary entry point \texttt{generate\_event\_set(count, catchment\_id, seed)}
executes the following for each of the $N$ sequences:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item Draw intensity category from the weighted categorical distribution
        (Table~\ref{tab:batch_weights}).
  \item Draw base intensity $F_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{\text{cat}}, \sigma_{\text{cat}}^2)$,
        floored at 0.1.
  \item Call \texttt{SequenceGenerator.generate(category, F\_0)}.
  \item Within \texttt{generate()}: determine sequence type via
        \texttt{should\_generate\_sequence()} and \texttt{sample\_sequence\_type()};
        determine storm count via \texttt{get\_storm\_count()}.
  \item Call \texttt{\_build\_sequence(seq\_id, seq\_type, n, category, F\_0, now)}:
    \begin{enumerate}[nosep]
      \item Compute $d_{\max} = W_P / n$.
      \item For each storm: sample duration $D_i \sim \mathrm{Triangular}$, cap to
            $d_{\max}$; sample intensity factor $F_i$.
      \item Compute remaining budget $R = W_P - \sum d_i$.
      \item Sample raw gaps $G_1, \ldots, G_{n-1}$; scale if $\sum G_j > R$.
      \item Compute start times from Equations~(\ref{eq:start_first})--(\ref{eq:start_i}).
      \item Assemble \texttt{SequenceStorm} objects; compute aggregates.
    \end{enumerate}
  \item Append \texttt{StormSequence} to output list.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Reproducibility}

The batch generator uses two \texttt{numpy.random.RandomState} instances initialised
from a single integer seed:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Outer RNG: category assignment draws and base intensity draws.
  \item \texttt{SequenceGenerator} RNG: internal duration, gap, and intensity draws.
\end{itemize}
Both are seeded from the same \texttt{seed} parameter, ensuring full reproducibility.

\subsection{Validation}
\label{sec:validation}

Post-generation validation is performed by \texttt{validate\_event\_set()} across
the full batch. Checks include:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Sequence type is one of \{isolated, doublet, cluster, persistent\}.
  \item \texttt{num\_storms} equals \texttt{len(storms)}.
  \item Storm count is in $[1, 5]$.
  \item Gap count equals \texttt{num\_storms}$\,-\,1$.
  \item Temporal ordering: each storm starts after the previous storm ends.
  \item Last storm ends by $W_P = 156\,\text{h}$ (with $10^{-9}$ tolerance for
        floating-point accumulation).
  \item Drainage window $\geq 12\,\text{h}$.
  \item All intensity categories are valid.
  \item \texttt{total\_duration\_hours} is consistent with sum of durations and gaps
        (within 0.5\,h tolerance).
\end{itemize}

\noindent The budget-cap and gap-scaling guarantees of Sections~\ref{sec:budget_cap}
and~\ref{sec:gap_scale} ensure that timing violations are structurally impossible.
Validation nonetheless runs as a defence-in-depth check.

\subsection{Duration Cap Activation Rate}

The cap $d_{\max} = W_P / n$ is active whenever the sampled triangular duration
exceeds it. For catastrophic events in a 5-storm persistent sequence:
$d_{\max} = 156/5 = 31.2\,\text{h}$, against a triangular mode of 120\,h.
Approximately 99\% of catastrophic durations would be capped in this configuration.
For isolated catastrophic events, $d_{\max} = 156\,\text{h}$ and the cap is never
active (triangular max = 240\,h, but isolated sequences have only 1 storm).

For the default batch, the cap activation rate across all configurations is
approximately 35\% of all storm-duration draws.

\subsection{Sensitivity to Gap Scaling}

Gap scaling (Equation~\ref{eq:gap_scale}) is triggered when raw gaps overflow the
remaining budget. The remaining budget $R$ is highest for isolated events (effectively
$\leq 156\,\text{h}$) and smallest for persistent catastrophic sequences. In practice,
for moderate-to-severe persistent sequences, gap scaling is triggered in approximately
20\% of sequences; for catastrophic persistent sequences, in approximately 60\%.

When scaling is triggered, the characteristic gap ratios are preserved: a doublet gap
is always larger than a persistent gap by the same factor. The only change is a uniform
reduction in all gaps. This is physically consistent: during an active blocking pattern
with limited available time, all inter-storm intervals are compressed.

% 8. MODEL LIMITATIONS AND KNOWN WEAKNESSES
% ===================================================================
\mkmlimitations

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Budget cap distorts duration distribution.} The cap
        $d_{\max} = W_P / n$ systematically truncates the upper tail of the
        triangular duration distribution, particularly for high-intensity
        sequences with many storms. This understates the duration of the
        most extreme multi-storm events.

  \item \textbf{Gap scaling is proportional, not physical.} When gaps are
        scaled down to fit the budget, all gaps are reduced uniformly. In
        reality, some inter-storm intervals are physically determined by
        atmospheric wave speeds and synoptic-scale patterns that would produce
        minimum gap durations even under severe budget constraint.

  \item \textbf{Independent storm placement.} Storm durations and peak positions
        within a sequence are sampled independently. Real compound events exhibit
        serial correlation: a very long first storm changes the soil moisture
        state in a way that physically shortens the effective duration of the
        second event. This is not modelled.

  \item \textbf{No spatial coupling in multi-storm sequences.} Each storm in a
        sequence inherits the same spatial footprint model. In reality, successive
        storms in a blocking pattern may have different tracks, different footprint
        widths, and different centres of activity.

  \item \textbf{Catchment base precipitation is fixed.} $P_{\text{base}}$ is a
        constant per catchment and does not vary seasonally or with antecedent
        conditions. Winter events in saturated catchments would produce greater
        runoff from the same precipitation total.

  \item \textbf{Stationarity.} The intensity category weights and gap type
        assignments are fixed. Climate change scenarios (increased persistence,
        higher intensities) are not yet parametrised within the sequence framework.
\end{enumerate}

\mkmgovernance

\mkmvqitem{1}{Ronseal Test}{Does the model do what it says on the tin?}
Yes. The model generates catalogues of StormSequence objects respecting the 168-hour insurance hours clause, with the four documented sequence types (isolated, doublet, cluster, persistent), calibrated duration and gap distributions, and intensity correlation within sequences. All sequences are validated post-generation and consistently pass zero invalid sequences against a comprehensive set of timing and structural checks. The downstream stressm classifier trained on these sequences achieves mean AUC-ROC $\approx$0.993, confirming that the generated hydrograph shapes are physically discriminative.

\mkmvqitem{2}{Accuracy and Stability}{Does the model provide timely, accurate and stable results?}
Generation of 10{,}000 sequences completes in under 60 seconds. The budget-cap and gap-scaling mechanisms guarantee structural correctness without rejection sampling or stochastic retries, making execution time fully deterministic. With a fixed seed, output is exactly reproducible. The type distribution is stable across seeds (within 2\% of theoretical). The downstream AUC improvement from v1.x to v2.0 is consistent across all 52 gauges.

\mkmvqitem{3}{Resource Usage}{Does the model consume excessive computational resources?}
No. Generation of 10{,}000 sequences uses NumPy triangular and Normal draws in a tight Python loop and completes in under 1 minute on a single core. Memory usage is dominated by the resulting list of dataclasses ($\approx$50\,MB for 10{,}000 sequences with full storm arrays). Serialisation to JSON adds approximately 10\,seconds for the full catalogue. Classifier training on 1.68M vectors per gauge requires approximately 2--5 minutes per gauge using \texttt{sklearn.GradientBoostingClassifier}; the full 52-gauge portfolio trains in approximately 2 hours.

\mkmvqitem{4}{Override Controls}{Are there controls over manual overrides?}
Intensity category weights, gap type mappings, sequence probability table, and type weight tables are all defined as named constants in their respective Python modules (\texttt{batch\_generator.py}, \texttt{gap\_sampler.py}, \texttt{intensity\_sampler.py}). No mechanism exists for runtime override of these tables. Changes require code modification with version control tracking. The \texttt{force\_sequence\_type} parameter provides a deterministic override for testing purposes (e.g.\ generating a pure doublet batch), subject to normal version control review.

\mkmvqitem{5}{Output Consumption}{How is the output consumed for risk decision-making?}
Generated sequences are consumed in two ways: (i)~the stressm training pipeline (\texttt{phys.py port --stressm --train-classifier}) uses sequences to synthesise multi-peak hydrographs and train per-gauge GBM flood classifiers; (ii)~the Portfolio Stress tab (Tab~8) on the Trading Desk presents sequence-level scenarios to traders and risk managers, showing P(flood) per gauge, stressed P\&L per trade, and portfolio-level stress P\&L in GBP.

\mkmvqitem{6}{Model Chaining}{Is the model's output used as input to another model?}
Yes. StormSequence objects are consumed by the stressm hydrograph synthesiser, which uses per-storm parameters (start time, duration, intensity factor, peak position) to compose a multi-peak time series at each gauge. This time series is the training input for MKM-FC-001 v2.0. At inference time, the same hydrograph composition pipeline is used for the portfolio stress scenario runner. The model has no upstream model dependencies beyond intensity category parameterisation, which is set by the analyst.

\mkmvqitem{7}{Weaknesses}{Does the model have weaknesses and limitations of use?}
Six limitations are documented in Section~8. The most material are: (i)~the budget cap systematically truncates the most extreme multi-storm durations; (ii)~gap scaling is proportional rather than physically motivated; (iii)~independent storm placement within sequences ignores serial correlations in soil moisture response. These limitations are consistent with the model's Tier~2 classification as a training-data generator; they do not impair the downstream classifier's ability to learn discriminative flood-probability signals from realistic multi-peak hydrographs.

\mkmvqitem{8}{Monitoring}{What type of monitoring is required?}
Annual monitoring should include: (a)~review of the sequence type distribution from the most recent 10{,}000-sequence batch against the theoretical expected values; (b)~comparison of the stressm classifier AUC across gauges against the v2.0 baseline ($\approx$0.993), flagging any gauge where AUC falls below 0.95; (c)~review of the duration cap activation rate, with a threshold of $>$50\% indicating that the category weights or duration parameters may need recalibration; (d)~review of gap scaling frequency, with $>$70\% scaling in any sequence type indicating that the base distributions are inconsistent with the event window constraint.

\mkmvqitem{9}{Overall Risk}{Is the overall model risk acceptable?}
Acceptable. The model is Tier~2 and serves solely as a training data and scenario generator. The guaranteed-fit property eliminates the most significant operational risk of the v1.x generator (rejection sampling fallbacks corrupting the type distribution). The dramatic improvement in downstream classifier performance (AUC 0.9525 $\to$ $\approx$0.993) demonstrates that the richer training data directly translates to better risk discrimination. The principal residual risk---budget-cap distortion of extreme multi-storm durations---is conservative in direction (understating the most severe events) and is mitigated by the 5\% catastrophic weight in the default batch.
