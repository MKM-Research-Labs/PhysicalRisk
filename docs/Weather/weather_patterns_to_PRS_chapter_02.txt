Chapter 2: Weather Data (pp. 53-86)
================================================================

CORE THESIS
-----------
Weather data is the raw material for the entire PRS framework, but banks should NOT
consume it directly. The chapter provides a comprehensive survey of operational NWP
systems (HRRR, ECMWF, GFS), their tradeoffs, data formats, and standardisation,
before concluding that the connection between atmospheric science and credit spreads
requires a disciplined translation pipeline. Weather data speaks in Kelvin, Pascals,
and kg/m2/s; banks speak in credit spreads and default probabilities. The purpose of
subsequent chapters is to bridge these two worlds.

KEY ARGUMENTS
-------------
1. Weather vs Climate Distinction
   - Weather: state of atmosphere at a specific time and place (hours-days)
   - Climate: statistics of weather variables over 30-year averages
   - This distinction matters because banks need near-term event distributions
     (weather-scale), not century-scale climate projections

2. The Three NWP Systems (Hierarchical Approach)

   HRRR (High-Resolution Rapid Refresh):
   - 3km resolution, hourly updates, convection-allowing
   - Covers CONUS only, 18-48 hour forecast horizon
   - ~2 million surface grid points, 50 elevation levels
   - Assimilates radar data every 15 minutes
   - Primary data source for the book's flood risk framework
   - ~100MB per forecast hour in GRIB2 format

   ECMWF (European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts):
   - 9km global resolution (TCo1279 cubic octahedral grid)
   - 137 vertical levels, 10-15 day forecast horizon
   - 51-member ensemble (ENS) for probabilistic forecasting
   - 4D-Var data assimilation: 800M observations daily, 60M assimilated
   - AIFS (AI Forecasting System) went operational Feb 2025
   - Consistently the most accurate medium-range forecaster globally
   - Hurricane Sandy 2012: only centre to predict the westward turn

   GFS (Global Forecast System):
   - 13km global resolution, 16-day forecast horizon
   - 127 vertical levels, 31-member ensemble (GEFS)
   - Completely free, unrestricted public access (key differentiator)
   - Trails ECMWF by ~1-2 days in forecast skill
   - Strong tropical cyclone track prediction
   - FV3 dynamical core since 2019

3. The Fundamental NWP Tradeoffs
   - Resolution vs computational cost (halving grid = 100x compute)
   - Domain coverage vs spatial detail
   - Forecast horizon vs update frequency
   - No single system optimises for all three

4. Data Formats: GRIB2 vs Zarr
   - GRIB2: traditional standard, ~100MB per file, must download entire domain
   - Zarr: cloud-optimised, chunked storage, ~40x more efficient for subsetting
   - Both available via NOAA Big Data Programme on AWS
   - Zarr enables selective extraction (e.g., 1MB vs 140MB for Alabama temp)

5. AI/ML Weather Prediction
   - ECMWF AIFS: 10x faster, 1000x less energy than physics-based models
   - NVIDIA Earth-2/CorrDiff: downscales 25km GEFS to 3km HRRR-equivalent
   - 1000x faster and 3000x less energy than conventional models
   - StormCast: ~10% more accurate than operational HRRR for extreme events
   - Operational adoption: Weather Company, Taiwan CWA, energy companies

6. WMO Standardisation
   - Universal parameter definitions across all NWP systems globally
   - 175+ nations, standardised instruments and methods
   - Enables seamless comparison and integration across systems
   - Banks can "stand on the shoulders of meteorological giants"

FRAMEWORKS AND STANDARDS INTRODUCED
------------------------------------
- WMO standardised atmospheric parameters (temperature, pressure, humidity, wind,
  precipitation, radiation, surface/boundary layer variables)
- GRIB2 and Zarr data formats
- ERA5 Reanalysis (1940-present, 31km, 137 levels)
- NOAA Big Data Programme (cloud-native access)
- Herbie Python package (programmatic NWP access via xarray)

THE CRITICAL CLOSING ARGUMENT (pp. 85-86)
------------------------------------------
The chapter's most important section for Chapter 22 is the closing: "Weather Data and
the Banking Risk Architecture Challenge." It makes the explicit argument that:

- Banks should NOT position themselves as consumers of raw weather data
- The expertise to convert atmospheric forecasts into credit risk metrics (GIS,
  hydrology, damage assessment, actuarial analysis) falls outside core banking
  competencies
- Building infrastructure to ingest weather data streams would burden risk
  architectures with information that rarely translates directly into actionable
  risk management decisions
- "A borrower's creditworthiness changes when their factory floods, not when
  precipitation rates exceed certain thresholds in GRIB2 files"
- The connection between weather data and credit spreads is sparse because the
  causal chain passes through physical damage, business interruption, and financial
  distress -- intermediate steps that weather models do not directly quantify

This is a crucial framing: it establishes that the translation pipeline (Chapters 3-8)
is what creates banking-usable outputs from weather data. Banks consume the translated
outputs, not the raw data.

WHAT THIS CHAPTER DEMANDS OF CHAPTER 22
-----------------------------------------
1. Chapter 22 does NOT need to show banks ingesting raw weather data. The chapter
   explicitly argues against this. Instead, Chapter 22 must show banks consuming
   translated outputs: hazard curves, flood probabilities, PRS spreads.

2. The data standardisation argument (WMO, GRIB2, Zarr) supports the integration
   thesis: the raw data layer is already standardised and interoperable. The
   translation pipeline adds CDM standardisation on top.

3. The hierarchical NWP approach (global context -> regional detail -> reanalysis
   for training) maps to a service architecture where a specialist provider runs
   the weather-to-flood pipeline and delivers CDM-formatted outputs to banks.
   This is the same model as Markit providing CDS pricing data -- banks don't
   run their own credit models from raw corporate filings.

4. ECMWF licensing complexities for commercial/financial product use are flagged
   as a consideration. GFS's free access removes this barrier. Chapter 22 should
   note that the data feed for PRS can use freely available sources.

5. The AI acceleration (CorrDiff, AIFS) dramatically reduces the computational
   cost of high-resolution weather prediction, making the translation pipeline
   economically viable at scale -- a point Chapter 22 can cite when addressing
   operational cost concerns.

INTEGRATION COST SIGNALS
-------------------------
- Standard: WMO standardisation means weather data is already interoperable
- Standard: GRIB2/Zarr formats well-supported by open-source tooling
- Standard: All three NWP systems are operational, validated, and accessible
- Novel: Banks should NOT build weather data infrastructure -- they need the
  translation layer (the book's core contribution)
- Novel: ECMWF licensing may constrain commercial use
- Key insight: competitive advantage in banking has never been in raw data
  access but in analytical infrastructure built on top of it
