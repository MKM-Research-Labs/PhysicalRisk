Chapter 3: Probabilistic Weather (pp. 87-97)
================================================================

CORE THESIS
-----------
Banks do not need deterministic weather forecasts. They need continuously evolving
probability distributions over weather outcomes -- what the book calls a "living
distribution." This replaces the static, quarterly scenario-based approach that
dominates current banking climate risk practice. Instead of freezing a handful of
plausible futures once per quarter, the framework maintains an evolving probability
distribution over thousands of physically realistic weather trajectories that updates
as new observations arrive.

The chapter is the conceptual overview for the five technical sub-chapters (3.1-3.5)
that follow. It establishes the narrative without formal equations.

KEY ARGUMENTS
-------------
1. Why Probabilistic (Not Deterministic)
   - A single forecast ("50mm of rain on Thursday") hides the fact that many other
     outcomes are almost equally plausible
   - Scenario-based approaches are "simple but brittle" -- cannot answer questions
     like "what is the chance a 1-in-100 year event occurs over this catchment in
     the next five days?"
   - The most consequential events are precisely those that almost never appear in
     the historical record

2. From Static Ensembles to Living Distributions
   - Operational ensembles (e.g., ECMWF's 51 members) are static snapshots
   - Banking applications need something dynamic -- probability changes materially
     within hours as new data arrives
   - "A framework that simply regenerates a fresh ensemble from scratch at each
     update wastes information and offers no coherent link between what we believed
     this morning and what we believe now"
   - Solution: Bayesian updating where the posterior becomes the prior for the next
     observation cycle

3. Physics-Informed Scenarios
   - Scenarios must be both diverse enough to explore genuine uncertainty AND
     constrained enough to remain meteorologically realistic
   - Conservation laws (mass, energy, momentum, moisture) filter out "impossible
     weather"
   - Hierarchical Bayesian extremes pool information across spatial scales to
     estimate tail probabilities even when local observations are sparse

4. Scenario Families as Banking-Usable Output
   - Thousands of trajectories reduced to a small number of scenario families:
     normal/non-disruptive, frontal (widespread manageable rain), slow-moving
     convective (high flood risk)
   - Probability weights carry through: "15% chance of slow-moving convective
     pattern over the next 72 hours"
   - This is the form in which probabilistic weather begins to look like a familiar
     risk input for trading desks

5. The Five-Phase Architecture (Roadmap)
   - 3.1 Initialization: physics-informed priors, conservation constraints,
     hierarchical Bayesian extremes
   - 3.2 Stochastic Evolution: SDE with drift (physics) and diffusion (uncertainty),
     Euler-Maruyama integration
   - 3.3 Sampling and Dependence: Sobol quasi-Monte Carlo, EOF dimension reduction,
     vine copulas
   - 3.4 Bayesian Updating: Ensemble Kalman Filter, continuous learning loop
   - 3.5 Precipitation Mapping: catchment-scale rainfall, IDF curves, scenario
     families

FORWARD REFERENCES
------------------
- Rainfall time series output feeds "into flood models and, through them, into
  credit and capital metrics"
- Living distribution "can be wired directly into risk systems" for intraday
  collateral valuation updates and hedging decisions via PRS or parametric insurance
- Scenario families are "the critical deliverable for banking risk systems"

CDS INFRASTRUCTURE ANALOGY
---------------------------
Strong: A risk desk monitoring flood exposure can watch the distribution evolve just
as a credit desk watches CDS spreads.

Strained: CDS spreads are single numbers updated by market activity; a living
distribution over thousands of weather trajectories is a fundamentally higher-
dimensional object. Scenario families are the dimensionality-reduction device that
makes the output look more like a conventional risk input.
