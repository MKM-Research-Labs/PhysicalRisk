Chapter 1: Physical Risk in Finance (pp. 30-52)
================================================================

CORE THESIS
-----------
Physical risk has moved from a peripheral concern to a central challenge in banking,
yet the tools for managing it remain primitive. The chapter establishes the problem
statement for the entire book: a USD 180 billion annual insurance protection gap,
accelerating natural catastrophe losses (5-7% annually since 1992), and a regulatory
landscape that demands quantification but lacks the model governance standards to
deliver it. The argument is that physical risk must be transformed from an unquantified
threat into a tradeable, manageable element of banking risk -- analogous to how credit
risk was made tradeable through CDS.

KEY ARGUMENTS
-------------
1. The Scale of the Problem
   - 2024: USD 320 billion economic losses, USD 140 billion insured (44% coverage)
   - Protection gap: ~USD 180 billion annually
   - Hydrometeorological disasters increased fivefold between 1971-80 and 2001-10
   - NGFS Phase V: 15% GDP losses by 2050 under current policies (3x prior estimate)
   - 70% of European bank corporate credit exposures directed to high-emitting sectors
   - EUR 630 billion potential banking sector losses under combined physical + transition stress

2. The Temporal Mismatch
   - Climate models project to 2100; banks lend on 3-7 year horizons; mortgages span 30 years
   - Insurance models calibrated for annual terms are unsuitable for multi-year banking exposures
   - The solution: reframe from "what will happen in 2100" to "what distribution of precipitation
     events might we experience through 2030"
   - Shift from deterministic scenarios to probabilistic near-term distributions

3. The Six-Step EBITDA Framework (and its failure)
   - Traditional: Precipitation -> Flood depth -> Physical damage -> Business interruption
     -> Revenue loss -> EBITDA impact
   - Each step compounds uncertainty; error bounds dwarf the estimate itself
   - Individual facility prediction is impossible; portfolio-level pattern recognition is achievable
   - The paradox: the framework fails at facility level but succeeds at portfolio level through
     statistical aggregation

4. The Vendor Disclosure Trap
   - GARP Risk Institute benchmarking (Oct 2025): 13 vendors, significant dispersion in
     hazard and damage estimates for the same properties
   - Vendors trace model capabilities to a few academic models (Saint-Venant) and public
     datasets (NOAA) -- dispersion comes from implementation, not theory
   - Vendors confuse testing with validation: passing code tests is not the same as
     demonstrating methodological appropriateness
   - SR 11-7 nine fundamental questions that vendors cannot answer due to opacity

5. The Basel Pillar Progression Paradox
   - Pillar 3 disclosures mandatory in Europe (EBA ITS), voluntary internationally (June 2025)
   - Pillar 2 progress has stalled: fewer than half of institutions include climate scenarios
   - Pillar 1 remains absent for physical risk
   - The progression (disclosure -> supervision -> capital) is blocked by "fundamental
     methodological shortcomings"
   - PRS is positioned as the market-based mechanism that bypasses this blockage

6. Bank of England SS4/25 Critique
   - Replaces SS3/19; genuine advances in governance, risk appetite, accountability
   - Critical blind spot: no model governance framework for hydrology, damage functions,
     or collateral impairment methodologies
   - Creates supervisory arbitrage: three banks can use entirely different methods,
     all compliant, none comparable
   - Without quantitative benchmarks, supervisors cannot challenge implausible results
   - SS4/25 is "process-heavy, judgment-intensive" but institutionalises methodological
     incoherence

FRAMEWORKS AND STANDARDS INTRODUCED
------------------------------------
- Four-step cat-modelling framework (hazard, exposure, vulnerability, loss) -- inherited baseline
- NGFS Phase V scenarios
- Basel III/IV physical risk capital requirements
- SR 11-7 model risk management guidance (2011)
- EBA Implementing Technical Standards for climate disclosure
- BCBS 530 (18 principles for climate-related financial risks)
- Bank of England SS4/25 (replacing SS3/19)
- TCFD framework (referenced)
- FINOS / CDM (introduced as the standardisation solution)

WHAT THIS CHAPTER DEMANDS OF CHAPTER 22
-----------------------------------------
Chapter 1 establishes three obstacles that Chapter 22 must resolve:

1. The vendor governance gap: vendors cannot provide SR 11-7-grade validation artefacts.
   Chapter 22 must show that the MKM framework provides transparent, governable models
   with full documentation, parameter inventories, and test evidence -- closing the gap
   that blocks Pillar 2 progression.

2. The temporal mismatch: Chapter 22 must show that PRS creates short-term, tradeable
   instruments (1-5 year tenors) that align with banking decision horizons, bypassing
   the 2100-horizon problem entirely.

3. The Basel Pillar Progression Paradox: Chapter 22 must show that PRS, priced via CDS
   mechanics on existing infrastructure, produces market-based risk metrics that satisfy
   Pillar 1 capital treatment without requiring the methodological consensus that Pillar 2
   climate stress testing cannot achieve.

The chapter's strongest signal to the reader: competitive advantage in banking has never
resided in the models (Black-Scholes is public domain) but in their implementation.
Physical risk vendors who hide behind proprietary methods are fighting the wrong battle.
This directly supports Chapter 22's argument that the science is open, the infrastructure
is shared (FINOS/CDM/ISDA), and the integration cost is implementation, not invention.
